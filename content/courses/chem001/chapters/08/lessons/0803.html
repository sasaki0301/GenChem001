Differentiating Polar and Nonpolar Compounds

Real-World Scenario

Why does water dissolve table salt but cooking oil does not? Why are plastics waterproof while sugar easily dissolves in water? Why does carbon dioxide have polar bonds but behave as a nonpolar molecule? The answers depend on molecular polarity. The distribution of electrons within chemical bonds and the overall shape of molecules determine whether compounds are polar or nonpolar. Molecular polarity greatly influences solubility, intermolecular forces, boiling points, biological activity, and engineering applications.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain bond polarity using electronegativity.
  • Differentiate polar and nonpolar covalent bonds.
  • Describe dipole moments.
  • Explain how molecular geometry affects polarity.
  • Determine whether molecules are polar or nonpolar.
  • Relate polarity to physical properties and applications.

Review: Electronegativity

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond.

The greater the electronegativity difference between two atoms, the more unevenly electrons are shared.

Recall from Chapter 7 that fluorine is the most electronegative element.

Bond Polarity

A covalent bond may be either nonpolar or polar depending on the difference in electronegativity between the bonded atoms.

Electronegativity Difference Bond Type
0 – 0.4 Nonpolar Covalent
0.5 – 1.7 Polar Covalent
> 1.7 Usually Ionic

Nonpolar Covalent Bonds

In a nonpolar covalent bond, electrons are shared equally because both atoms have similar electronegativities.

Examples:
  • H₂
  • N₂
  • O₂
  • Cl₂
H—H

Neither atom pulls electrons more strongly.

Polar Covalent Bonds

A polar covalent bond forms when one atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly than the other.

Electrons spend more time near the more electronegative atom.
Examples:
  • H—Cl
  • O—H
  • C—O

Partial Charges

Unequal sharing of electrons produces partial charges.

δ⁺ ——→ δ⁻

The arrow points toward the more electronegative atom.

Example:
Hδ⁺ — Oδ⁻

Dipole Moment

A dipole moment measures the separation of positive and negative charge within a bond.

Every polar bond possesses a dipole moment.

Bond Polarity vs Molecular Polarity

Bond Polarity Molecular Polarity
Depends on one bond. Depends on the entire molecule.
Controlled by electronegativity. Controlled by electronegativity and molecular shape.

How Molecular Geometry Affects Polarity

Even if individual bonds are polar, the molecule itself may be nonpolar if the bond dipoles cancel each other.

Always determine the molecular geometry before deciding whether a molecule is polar.

Example 1 — Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)

O=C=O
  • Polar C=O bonds
  • Linear geometry
  • Dipoles cancel

Overall Molecule: Nonpolar

Example 2 — Water (H₂O)

H—O—H
  • Polar O—H bonds
  • Bent geometry
  • Dipoles do not cancel

Overall Molecule: Polar

Example 3 — Methane (CH₄)

  • Symmetrical tetrahedral geometry
  • Bond dipoles cancel

Overall Molecule: Nonpolar

Example 4 — Ammonia (NH₃)

  • Trigonal pyramidal geometry
  • Lone pair creates asymmetry
  • Dipoles do not cancel

Overall Molecule: Polar

Step-by-Step Guide to Determining Polarity

  1. Draw the Lewis structure.
  2. Determine the molecular geometry using VSEPR.
  3. Identify whether the bonds are polar.
  4. Determine whether the bond dipoles cancel.
  5. Classify the molecule as polar or nonpolar.

Check Your Understanding

1. What determines bond polarity?

The difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms.

2. Is H₂ polar or nonpolar?

Nonpolar.

3. Why is CO₂ nonpolar even though its C=O bonds are polar?

Its linear geometry causes the bond dipoles to cancel.

4. Why is H₂O polar?

Its bent geometry prevents the bond dipoles from canceling.

5. Which molecule is polar: NH₃ or CH₄?

NH₃ is polar; CH₄ is nonpolar.

6. What are the four steps used to determine molecular polarity?

Draw the Lewis structure, determine geometry, identify bond polarity, and determine whether the dipoles cancel.

Lesson Summary

  • Bond polarity depends on differences in electronegativity.
  • Polar covalent bonds share electrons unequally, while nonpolar covalent bonds share electrons equally.
  • Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular geometry.
  • Symmetrical molecules often have canceling dipoles and are nonpolar.
  • Asymmetrical molecules usually possess a net dipole and are polar.
  • Polarity influences solubility, intermolecular forces, and many physical and chemical properties.
