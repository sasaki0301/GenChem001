Chemical Bonding Exercises

Real-World Scenario

Engineers, chemists, pharmacists, and materials scientists rarely solve one chemistry concept at a time. Instead, they must determine how atoms bond, draw molecular structures, predict shapes, and explain physical properties—all from a chemical formula. This lesson serves as an integrated practice session that combines everything learned throughout this chapter. By completing progressively challenging exercises, you will strengthen your understanding of chemical bonding and prepare for the chapter assessment.

Learning Objectives

  • Classify compounds as ionic or covalent.
  • Predict ions formed by common elements.
  • Draw Lewis structures correctly.
  • Determine molecular geometry using VSEPR Theory.
  • Determine molecular polarity.
  • Apply bonding concepts to real-world situations.

Part F — Integrated Challenge Problems

Problem 1
Magnesium reacts with chlorine.

  • Bond type: Ionic
  • Ions formed: Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻
  • Chemical formula: MgCl₂

Problem 2
Determine the Lewis structure, molecular geometry, and polarity of carbon dioxide.

  • Lewis Structure: O=C=O
  • Geometry: Linear
  • Polarity: Nonpolar

Problem 3
Determine the Lewis structure, molecular geometry, and polarity of ammonia.

  • Lewis Structure: Three N–H single bonds with one lone pair on N
  • Geometry: Trigonal pyramidal
  • Polarity: Polar

Problem 4
Why does water dissolve sodium chloride more effectively than cooking oil?

Water is a polar solvent. Its partially positive hydrogen atoms and partially negative oxygen atom attract and stabilize Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, allowing the ionic crystal to dissolve. Cooking oil is nonpolar and cannot effectively stabilize these ions, so sodium chloride does not dissolve in it.

Problem 5
Explain why methane (CH₄) is nonpolar even though each C–H bond is slightly polar.

Methane has a symmetrical tetrahedral geometry. The small bond dipoles are equal in magnitude and point toward the corners of the tetrahedron, causing them to cancel each other. As a result, methane has no net dipole moment and is classified as a nonpolar molecule.

Engineering Applications

Successful engineers frequently combine multiple chemical concepts simultaneously.
  • Selecting battery electrolytes requires understanding ionic compounds and polarity.
  • Drug design depends on molecular geometry and polarity.
  • Materials engineers use bonding type to predict hardness, conductivity, and melting point.
  • Environmental engineers predict contaminant movement using molecular polarity and solubility.

Lesson Summary

  • Ionic compounds form through electron transfer, while covalent compounds form through electron sharing.
  • Lewis structures represent the arrangement of atoms, bonding pairs, and lone pairs.
  • VSEPR Theory predicts molecular geometry based on electron-pair repulsion.
  • Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular geometry.
  • Chemists often apply all of these concepts together to predict the behavior of substances.
  • Mastering these integrated skills provides a strong foundation for future topics in chemistry and engineering.
