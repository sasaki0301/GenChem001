Chemical Bonding Exercises

Real-World Scenario

In real chemistry work, scientists rarely analyze only one idea at a time. They must classify bond type, predict ions, draw Lewis structures, determine molecular geometry, and explain polarity from unfamiliar formulas. This lesson is a guided mastery workshop. The examples are intentionally different from the previous lessons so you can apply chemical bonding concepts to new situations.

Learning Objectives

  • Classify unfamiliar compounds as ionic or covalent.
  • Predict common ions from periodic table position.
  • Draw Lewis structures for new molecules and ions.
  • Determine molecular geometry using VSEPR Theory.
  • Determine whether molecules are polar or nonpolar.
  • Apply bonding concepts to real-world chemical scenarios.

Part F — Integrated Case Studies

Case 1: A manufacturer needs a substance that dissolves readily in water to prepare an electrolyte solution. Which is the best choice?

  • CCl₄
  • Na₂SO₄
  • CH₄
  • CO₂
Best choice: Na₂SO₄

Na₂SO₄ is ionic and can dissociate into ions in water. Because the solution contains mobile ions, it can conduct electricity and function as an electrolyte.

Case 2: An engineer is selecting a nonpolar compound for a solvent system. Which compound is the best choice?

  • NH₃
  • SO₂
  • CCl₄
  • H₂O
Best choice: CCl₄

CCl₄ is tetrahedral and symmetrical. Although the C—Cl bonds are polar, the dipoles cancel, making the molecule nonpolar.

Case 3: A chemist synthesizes PCl₃. Determine its bond type, Lewis structure features, molecular geometry, and polarity.

  • Bond type: Covalent
  • Lewis structure: P is central with three P—Cl bonds and one lone pair on P
  • Electron groups: 4 around phosphorus
  • Molecular geometry: Trigonal pyramidal
  • Polarity: Polar

Case 4: Compare CO₂ and SO₂. Why is CO₂ nonpolar while SO₂ is polar, even though both contain polar bonds?

CO₂ is linear and symmetrical, so its bond dipoles cancel. SO₂ is bent because sulfur has a lone pair, so its bond dipoles do not cancel. Therefore, CO₂ is nonpolar while SO₂ is polar.

Case 5: A solvent is needed to dissolve ionic salts. Which would work best?

  • CCl₄
  • HCN
  • SO₃
  • H₂O
Best choice: H₂O

Water is highly polar and can stabilize positive and negative ions through ion-dipole attractions. This makes it effective for dissolving many ionic salts.

Challenge Reflection

When analyzing an unfamiliar compound, ask:
  1. Is it ionic or covalent?
  2. How many valence electrons are present?
  3. What is the correct Lewis structure?
  4. How many electron groups surround the central atom?
  5. What is the molecular geometry?
  6. Are the bonds polar?
  7. Do the dipoles cancel?

Lesson Summary

  • Ionic compounds usually form between metals and nonmetals.
  • Covalent compounds usually form between nonmetals.
  • Lewis structures help identify bonding pairs, lone pairs, and resonance possibilities.
  • VSEPR Theory predicts molecular geometry from electron group arrangement.
  • Molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular shape.
  • Symmetrical molecules often have canceling dipoles and are nonpolar.
  • Asymmetrical molecules often have a net dipole and are polar.
  • Analyzing unfamiliar compounds strengthens chemical reasoning and prepares students for higher-level chemistry.
