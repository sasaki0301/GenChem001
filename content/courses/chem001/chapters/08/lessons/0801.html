Recognizing How Ionic and Covalent Bonds are Formed

Real-World Scenario

Why is table salt (NaCl) hard and able to conduct electricity when dissolved in water, while sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) dissolves but does not conduct electricity? Why is diamond one of the hardest known materials, while graphite—made entirely of carbon—is soft enough to write with? The answers lie in the way atoms bond with one another. Chemical bonding determines the structure, properties, and behavior of every substance around us. Understanding how bonds form helps explain why materials differ in strength, conductivity, melting point, and countless other properties.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why atoms form chemical bonds.
  • Describe the Octet Rule.
  • Relate valence electrons to chemical bonding.
  • Differentiate ionic and covalent bonding.
  • Illustrate electron transfer and electron sharing.
  • Predict whether elements form ionic or covalent compounds.
  • Identify common ionic and covalent compounds found in everyday life.

Why Do Atoms Form Chemical Bonds?

Most atoms are not stable by themselves because their outermost energy level (valence shell) is incomplete. To become more stable, atoms interact with other atoms by gaining, losing, or sharing electrons.

Atoms form chemical bonds in order to achieve a lower-energy, more stable electron configuration.

Many atoms become stable when they obtain an electron configuration similar to the nearest noble gas.

The Octet Rule

The Octet Rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons until they have eight electrons in their outermost shell.

Stable atoms generally possess eight valence electrons.

Examples

Element Valence Electrons Needs
Na 1 Lose 1 electron
Mg 2 Lose 2 electrons
Cl 7 Gain 1 electron
O 6 Gain 2 electrons
Ne 8 Already stable

Valence Electrons and Bonding

Valence electrons are the electrons located in the highest occupied energy level. These electrons participate directly in chemical bonding.

The number of valence electrons determines how an atom bonds with other atoms.

Examples

Element Electron Configuration Valence Electrons
Na [Ne] 3s¹ 1
O [He] 2s²2p⁴ 6
Cl [Ne] 3s²3p⁵ 7
C [He] 2s²2p² 4

Types of Chemical Bonds

Two major types of chemical bonds are encountered in General Chemistry.

Bond Type How It Forms
Ionic Bond Transfer of electrons
Covalent Bond Sharing of electrons

Ionic Bonding

An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers one or more electrons to another atom. This usually occurs between a metal and a nonmetal.

Metal → loses electrons → positive ion (cation)

Nonmetal → gains electrons → negative ion (anion)

The attraction between oppositely charged ions forms an ionic bond.

Example 1: Formation of Sodium Chloride

Step 1

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Step 2

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Step 3

Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

Sodium transfers one electron to chlorine. Both atoms achieve stable electron configurations.

Example 2: Formation of Magnesium Oxide

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻

O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻

Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → MgO

Magnesium loses two electrons while oxygen gains two electrons.

Characteristics of Ionic Compounds

Property Description
Composition Metal + Nonmetal
Bond Formation Electron transfer
Melting Point Usually high
Electrical Conductivity Conducts when molten or dissolved
Structure Crystal lattice

Covalent Bonding

A covalent bond forms when atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.

Covalent bonding usually occurs between nonmetals.

Instead of transferring electrons, both atoms share electrons to complete their valence shells.

Single, Double, and Triple Bonds

Bond Type Shared Electron Pairs
Single Bond 1 pair
Double Bond 2 pairs
Triple Bond 3 pairs

Example 3: Hydrogen Molecule

H· + ·H → H:H

Each hydrogen shares one electron, producing a stable duet.

Example 4: Water Molecule

Oxygen has six valence electrons and needs two more.

H : O : H

Each hydrogen shares one electron with oxygen.

Example 5: Carbon Dioxide

O = C = O

Carbon forms two double bonds with oxygen atoms.

Example 6: Nitrogen Molecule

N ≡ N

Nitrogen atoms share three pairs of electrons, producing a triple bond.

Characteristics of Covalent Compounds

Property Description
Composition Nonmetal + Nonmetal
Bond Formation Electron sharing
Melting Point Usually lower than ionic compounds
Electrical Conductivity Poor conductor
Examples H₂O, CO₂, CH₄

Ionic vs Covalent Bonding

Property Ionic Covalent
Occurs Between Metal + Nonmetal Nonmetal + Nonmetal
Electron Behavior Transferred Shared
Particles Formed Ions Molecules
Electrical Conductivity Good when dissolved Poor
Examples NaCl, MgO H₂O, CO₂, NH₃

Check Your Understanding

1. Why do atoms form chemical bonds?

To achieve a more stable, lower-energy electron configuration.

2. How many valence electrons does chlorine have?

7 valence electrons.

3. What type of bond forms between sodium and chlorine?

An ionic bond.

4. What type of bond forms between hydrogen and oxygen in water?

A covalent bond.

5. Which atom loses electrons during ionic bonding?

The metal atom.

6. How many electron pairs are shared in a double bond?

Two pairs.

7. Which bond is present in nitrogen gas (N₂)?

A triple covalent bond.

8. Predict the bond type formed between magnesium and oxygen.

Ionic bond because magnesium is a metal and oxygen is a nonmetal.

9. Predict the bond type formed between carbon and oxygen.

Covalent bond because both are nonmetals.

10. Explain why NaCl conducts electricity when dissolved but sugar does not.

NaCl dissociates into mobile ions in solution, allowing electrical current to flow. Sugar remains as neutral molecules and therefore does not conduct electricity.

Lesson Summary

  • Atoms form chemical bonds to achieve more stable electron configurations.
  • The Octet Rule explains why many atoms gain, lose, or share electrons.
  • Ionic bonds involve electron transfer between metals and nonmetals.
  • Covalent bonds involve electron sharing between nonmetals.
  • Single, double, and triple bonds differ in the number of shared electron pairs.
  • Ionic and covalent compounds exhibit distinct physical and chemical properties.
  • Chemical bonding explains the structure and behavior of countless materials used in engineering and everyday life.
