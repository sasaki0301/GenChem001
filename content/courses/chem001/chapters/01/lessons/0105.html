Accuracy, Precision, and Errors

A balance gives nearly the same reading each time. Does that guarantee that its reading is correct? In this lesson, we will judge measurement results and decide how to improve them.

ObjectivesConceptsAnalyze dataErrors and improvementsPracticeKey takeaways

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish accuracy from precision using measurement data.
  • Compare repeated measurements with one another and with an appropriate reference value.
  • Identify possible systematic and random sources of error and propose practical improvements.
  • Calculate and interpret percent error when an accepted value is available.

1. Accuracy and precision

Accuracy

Accuracy describes how close a measurement is to the true value. Because the true value is usually unknown, a suitable accepted or reference value is often used for comparison.

Precision

Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree under stated conditions. A smaller spread generally indicates greater precision for comparable trials.

Repeated results can be close together yet all be displaced from the reference. Repeating a measurement helps evaluate consistency, but consistency alone does not establish accuracy.

Context matters: Whether a difference is acceptable depends on the instrument, uncertainty, and purpose. More displayed digits alone do not guarantee that a measurement is accurate or more precise.

2. Examine repeated measurements

Suppose a reference mass is 10.00 g. For this classroom comparison, treat an average within 0.05 g of the reference as close and a range of 0.05 g or less as tightly grouped. These cutoffs are illustrative, not universal standards.

Mean is the sum divided by the number of measurements. Range is the largest result minus the smallest result. In this simplified comparison, the mean helps assess the group’s closeness to the reference and range describes its spread.

Worked example · Set B

Mean = (9.49 + 9.50 + 9.51) ÷ 3 = 9.50 g. Range = 9.51 − 9.49 = 0.02 g.

The measurements are tightly grouped, but their mean is 0.50 g below the reference. They illustrate high precision with a mean far from the reference.

Interpret carefully: In Set C, the mean equals the reference even though the individual readings are widely spread. Some individual readings are far from the reference. A close mean may occur by cancellation and does not make every measurement accurate.

3. Sources of error and improvements

Measurement error is the difference between a measured value and a true or reference value. Error is not necessarily carelessness: it can arise from an instrument, technique, environmental conditions, or limits of a method.

Systematic and random effects

PatternExamplePossible improvement
Systematic: a consistent bias or directional shiftA balance with a zero offset reads 0.20 g high.Check the zero and calibrate or service the balance using appropriate standards.
Random: readings vary unpredictably from trial to trialSmall, changing vibrations cause fluctuations in a balance reading.Use a stable surface, reduce vibrations, and repeat independent measurements.
Changing techniqueA meniscus is read from a different angle on each trial.Read at eye level and use a consistent procedure.

Reading consistently from an incorrect angle can cause systematic bias; changing the viewing angle can add random variation. The same source can affect results differently depending on how the measurement is made.

Repeating measurements and averaging can reduce the effect of random variation, but averaging alone does not remove a consistent systematic bias. Check the method and instrument against suitable standards.

Apply it · Troubleshooting

A group measures a reference object three times: 19.78 g, 19.79 g, and 19.80 g. The reference value is 20.00 g. The results are close together but about 0.21 g low on average. A zero offset or calibration problem is one possible explanation; the data alone do not prove the cause. The group should check the balance zero and compare it with a suitable standard before repeating the measurements.

4. Percent error

When a suitable nonzero accepted value is available, percent error expresses the magnitude of the difference relative to that value:

Percent error = |measured value − accepted value| ÷ |accepted value| × 100%

Worked example

A sample’s accepted mass is 40.0 g and a student measures 39.2 g.

Difference = |39.2 − 40.0| = 0.8 g.

Percent error = (0.8 ÷ 40.0) × 100% = 2%.

The absolute value means the reported percent error is nonnegative. The original measurement is lower than the accepted value; that direction is lost in the absolute percent error.

Percent error is a useful comparison, but a small percentage alone does not tell us whether a result meets the accuracy needed for a particular task or whether the reference value itself is sufficiently reliable.

6. Key terms and takeaways

  • Accuracy: closeness to a true value, often assessed using a suitable reference.
  • Precision: closeness among repeated measurements under stated conditions.
  • Range: highest result minus lowest result; one simple indicator of spread.
  • Systematic bias: a consistent shift that repeated measurements alone cannot remove.
  • Random variation: unpredictable trial-to-trial differences that can often be reduced through improved technique and repeated measurements.
  • Percent error: absolute difference relative to a nonzero accepted value, multiplied by 100%.
