Scientific Notation

Chemistry connects the scale of atoms with enormous numbers of particles. Scientific notation gives us a compact way to express and calculate with both very small and very large values.

ObjectivesConceptsPracticeKey takeaways

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Identify the coefficient and exponent in scientific notation.
  • Convert numbers between ordinary form and scientific notation.
  • Multiply, divide, add, and subtract numbers expressed in scientific notation.
  • Use a calculator’s exponent function and check whether an answer is reasonable.

1. The structure of scientific notation

A nonzero number in normalized scientific notation has the form:

a × 10n, where 1 ≤ |a| < 10 and n is an integer

a is the coefficient and n is the exponent. The coefficient can be negative; its magnitude must be at least 1 and less than 10. Zero is usually written simply as 0.

−420−4.2 × 10²The sign of the number stays with its coefficient
The sign of the number and the sign of the exponent are different ideas. For example, 4.2 × 10⁻³ is a positive number.

2. Convert between forms

Ordinary form → scientific notation

  1. Move the decimal point until the coefficient’s magnitude is at least 1 and less than 10.
  2. Count the places moved. Moving left gives a positive exponent; moving right gives a negative exponent.
  3. Write the coefficient multiplied by 10 raised to that exponent.

Example 1 · A large value

74,500,000 → 7.45. The decimal moves seven places to the left, so 74,500,000 = 7.45 × 10⁷.

Example 2 · A small value

0.0000820 → 8.20. The decimal moves five places to the right, so 0.0000820 = 8.20 × 10⁻⁵. The final zero is retained to show the precision of the original measurement.

Scientific notation → ordinary form

Move the decimal point right for a positive exponent or left for a negative exponent. Add zeros as needed.

3.46 × 10⁴ = 34,600;   6.08 × 10⁻³ = 0.00608.

The ordinary form 34,600 does not clearly show whether its final zeros are significant; retain the scientific notation when that detail matters.

3. Calculations in scientific notation

Multiplication

Multiply coefficients and add exponents.

(2.0 × 10³)(3.0 × 10²) = 6.0 × 10⁵.

Division

Divide coefficients and subtract exponents.

(8.4 × 10⁶) ÷ (2.0 × 10²) = 4.2 × 10⁴.

Addition and subtraction

First rewrite the numbers with the same power of ten, then add or subtract their coefficients.

Example 3 · Matching exponents

2.4 × 10⁴ + 3.0 × 10³ = 2.4 × 10⁴ + 0.30 × 10⁴ = 2.70 × 10⁴.

Applying the decimal-place rule to the matched coefficients gives 2.7 × 10⁴. We will examine this rounding rule in Lesson 4.

Example 4 · Normalize the result

(6.0 × 10⁴)(4.0 × 10³) = 24 × 10⁷ = 2.4 × 10⁸. Adjust the exponent when changing the coefficient.

Calculator tip: Enter 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ using the calculator’s EXP, EE, or ×10ˣ key as directed by its manual. A display such as 3.2E−5 means 3.2 × 10⁻⁵. Avoid typing an extra “× 10” if the exponent key already supplies it.

Key terms and takeaways

  • Normalized scientific notation has the form a × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ |a| < 10 for nonzero values.
  • Positive and negative exponents indicate powers of ten; preserve the original value when converting.
  • For multiplication add exponents; for division subtract them.
  • For addition or subtraction, first match exponents.
  • Use significant-figure and decimal-place rules when reporting measured results.
