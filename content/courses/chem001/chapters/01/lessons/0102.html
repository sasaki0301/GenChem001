Density of Solids

A small metal object can have more mass than a much larger piece of foam. Density helps explain how much mass a material has for a given volume.

ObjectivesDensityCalculationsInvestigationPracticeKey takeaways

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain density as mass per unit volume and distinguish it from mass and volume.
  • Calculate the density of a solid using measured mass and volume, including volume found by displacement.
  • Compare densities and explain whether an object is expected to float or sink in a given liquid.
  • Organize measurements and report findings with appropriate units and a supported conclusion.
Before you begin: Recall from Lesson 1 that mass can be measured with a balance and that 1 mL = 1 cm³.

1. What is density?

Density describes how much mass is contained in a given volume. It relates two quantities: mass and volume.

Density = mass ÷ volume   |   ρ = m/V

The Greek letter ρ (rho) represents density. For solids, common units are g/cm³ and g/mL. Since 1 cm³ = 1 mL, a value in g/cm³ has the same numerical value in g/mL.

Compare equal volumes

Sample A has a mass of 12 g and a volume of 4 cm³; sample B has a mass of 20 g and the same volume. Their densities are 3 g/cm³ and 5 g/cm³, respectively. Sample B has more mass in each cubic centimeter, so it is denser.

For a uniform material at the same conditions, changing the sample size changes its mass and volume but not its density. Density may change with temperature, and measured values can be affected by composition or trapped air.

2. Calculate density step by step

  1. Identify the mass and volume of the same sample.
  2. Check that the units are appropriate and convert if needed.
  3. Divide mass by volume and include the resulting unit.
  4. Interpret your answer as mass in each unit of volume.

Example 1 · A rectangular block

A block has a mass of 48.0 g. It is 4.0 cm long, 2.0 cm wide, and 3.0 cm high. Find its density.

Step 1: Find its volume. V = 4.0 cm × 2.0 cm × 3.0 cm = 24 cm³.

Step 2: Divide mass by volume.

ρ = 48.0 g ÷ 24 cm³ = 2.0 g/cm³

For this calculation the result is reported to two significant figures, matching the least precise measured dimensions. Significant-figure rules will be studied in Lesson 4.

Example 2 · An irregular solid

A pebble has a mass of 21.6 g. A graduated cylinder reads 20.0 mL before the pebble is added and 28.0 mL after it is completely submerged.

Step 1: Find the pebble’s volume. V = 28.0 mL − 20.0 mL = 8.0 mL = 8.0 cm³.

Step 2: Find its density.

ρ = 21.6 g ÷ 8.0 mL = 2.7 g/mL

Each milliliter of this sample has a mass of approximately 2.7 g.

Watch for errors: In displacement, use the change in the water reading, not the final reading. Do not divide volume by mass when finding density.

Will it float or sink?

Compare an object’s overall density with the liquid’s density. If it is less dense than the liquid, it can float; if it is more dense, it sinks. Similar densities may allow neutral buoyancy. Shape and trapped air affect an object’s overall density, so a hollow object can float even when the solid material in its walls is denser than water.

For simple comparisons, use water’s approximate density of 1.0 g/mL near room temperature. An object with an overall density of 0.80 g/mL is expected to float in this water, while one at 2.7 g/mL is expected to sink.

3. Investigation: Measure and report density

Measure a suitable common solid and use your results to calculate its density. Work with your teacher’s instructions and the equipment available.

Materials: balance; ruler for a rectangular block, or a graduated cylinder and water for a suitable irregular solid; calculator; and a solid sample.

  1. Measure and record the sample’s mass in grams.
  2. Find its volume: multiply dimensions for a rectangular block, or subtract the initial water reading from the final reading for a fully submerged irregular object.
  3. Divide mass by volume. Record the density and unit.
  4. State one observation and explain whether the result indicates floating or sinking in water, if that comparison is applicable.
For displacement: Choose a solid that does not dissolve, react with, or significantly absorb water. Submerge it completely without trapped bubbles; avoid splashing. If it floats, use an appropriate holder and account for the holder’s displacement. Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.

Challenge · Evaluate a claim

A learner says, “The heavier object must always be denser.” Is this supported by mass alone?

Show answer

No. Density depends on both mass and volume. For example, 100 g occupying 200 cm³ has a density of 0.50 g/cm³, while 20 g occupying 5 cm³ has a density of 4 g/cm³. The lighter object is denser.

5. Key terms and takeaways

  • Density: mass per unit volume; ρ = m/V.
  • Common units: g/cm³ and g/mL; 1 cm³ = 1 mL.
  • Regular rectangular solids: calculate volume from length × width × height.
  • Irregular solids: where appropriate, calculate volume using final minus initial water readings.
  • Floating and sinking: compare the object’s overall density with that of the liquid.
  • Reporting: include the sample, measured values, calculation, units, and a conclusion supported by the data.
