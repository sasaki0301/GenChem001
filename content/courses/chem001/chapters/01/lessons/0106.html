Scientific notation

A nonzero value is written as a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ |a| < 10 and n is an integer. For example, 0.0000320 = 3.20 × 10⁻⁵. Multiply coefficients and add exponents for multiplication; divide coefficients and subtract exponents for division. Match exponents before adding or subtracting.

Significant figures and rounding

  • Nonzero digits and zeros between nonzero digits are significant; leading zeros are not.
  • Trailing zeros after a decimal are significant. Whole-number trailing zeros without a decimal can be ambiguous; scientific notation clarifies them.
  • For multiplication or division, report the result with the fewest significant figures among the measured inputs. Exact counts and defined conversions do not limit the result.
  • For addition or subtraction, round to the least precise place value. Retain extra digits during intermediate calculations and round at the end.

Accuracy and precision

Accuracy concerns closeness to the true value, usually assessed using a suitable reference. Precision concerns agreement among repeated measurements. A consistent bias may point to a systematic effect; unpredictable variation may be reduced through better technique and repeated measurements. Averages alone do not remove systematic bias.

2. Worked review: from measurement to conclusion

A learner measures a dry, nonreactive solid that does not absorb or dissolve in water. Its mass is 18.0 g. Water rises from 20.0 mL to 26.0 mL when the solid is fully submerged without bubbles.

Step 1 · Find volume

V = 26.0 − 20.0 = 6.0 mL = 6.0 cm³. The subtraction is reported to the tenths place.

Step 2 · Calculate density

ρ = 18.0 g ÷ 6.0 mL = 3.0 g/mL. The volume has two significant figures.

Step 3 · Interpret

The sample’s density is greater than the approximate density of water near room temperature, 1.0 g/mL, so it is expected to sink in water. The learner can report the mass, both water readings, calculated volume and density, units, and prediction.

Step 4 · Check measurement quality

Repeat independent measurements to assess spread; read the meniscus at eye level and check the balance with a suitable standard. A reference density for the same material, under comparable conditions, would be needed to evaluate closeness to an accepted density.

These values are an instructional example, not actual experimental results. A density comparison alone does not definitively identify an unknown material.

Challenge · Connect the ideas

A learner repeatedly measures the same reference mass and obtains 12.48 g, 12.49 g, and 12.50 g. The reference is 12.00 g. Describe the pattern and suggest a next step.

Show answer

The readings are tightly grouped (range 0.02 g) but are about 0.49 g above the reference on average. Check the balance zero and calibration with suitable standards, then repeat the measurements. A consistent instrument offset is a possible cause, not a conclusion proven by these data.

4. Before the chapter assessment

  • I can select units and instruments for mass and volume.
  • I can calculate a solid’s volume and density and explain what my result means.
  • I can convert and calculate using scientific notation.
  • I can apply significant-figure and place-value rules to measurements and calculations.
  • I can compare repeated results with each other and a suitable reference, identify possible errors, and propose improvements.
