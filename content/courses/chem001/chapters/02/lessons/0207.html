1. How the periodic table developed

Scientist Contribution Limit or development
Döbereiner Noticed selected triads of similar elements with an approximate atomic-mass relationship. Only some elements fitted the triad pattern.
Newlands Proposed the Law of Octaves after observing recurring properties in a mass-ordered sequence. The pattern did not fit all known elements.
Mendeleev Arranged elements mainly by atomic mass and properties; left gaps and predicted missing elements. Some placements did not follow strict increasing-mass order.
Moseley Provided evidence for atomic number as the organizing principle. The modern table is ordered by increasing atomic number.

Mendeleev’s prediction of eka-silicon was later compared with the properties of discovered germanium. Agreement provided evidence supporting the predictive value of the table.

2. Read the modern periodic table

Group

A vertical column, numbered 1–18. Members often have related chemical properties.

Period

A horizontal row, numbered 1–7. Atomic number increases across the row.

Family Group Examples
Alkali metals 1, excluding hydrogen Li, Na, K
Alkaline earth metals 2 Be, Mg, Ca
Halogens 17 F, Cl, Br
Noble gases 18 He, Ne, Ar

Atomic number is the number of protons and determines an element’s position. For example, chlorine is in group 17, period 3, and has atomic number 17. Hydrogen is a nonmetal, even though it is usually placed above group 1.

3. Compare periodic trends

The arrows show directions of general increase; they are guides, not scales or rules without exceptions.

Atomic radius

Relative atomic mass

Direction Atomic radius Relative atomic mass
Left to right across a period Generally decreases as effective nuclear attraction increases. Generally increases, with exceptions.
Down a group Generally increases as occupied shells and shielding increase. Generally increases.
Comparisons need care: Use neutral atomic radii measured with consistent definitions. Diagonal position alone may be inconclusive. Tellurium and iodine illustrate an atomic-mass exception: Te comes first by atomic number even though its listed relative atomic mass is slightly greater.

4. Shells, subshells, and orbitals

A shell groups orbitals with principal quantum number n. A subshell is labeled s, p, d, or f in this course. An orbital is described by a wavefunction and an electron probability distribution; it is not a fixed circular path.

Subshell Orbitals Maximum electrons First allowed
s 1 2 1s
p 3 6 2p
d 5 10 3d
f 7 14 4f

The maximum number of electrons in shell n is 2n²; for n = 3, the maximum is 18. This is capacity, not a statement that the shell is full or a universal filling order.

Allowed atomic energy states are discrete. A photon emitted during a transition from a higher to a lower state carries energy equal to the difference: Ephoton = Ehigher − Elower = hν. In the simplified hydrogen model, orbitals with the same n share an energy; multielectron subshell energies can differ and overlap.

Electron configuration

For a neutral atom, electron count equals atomic number. In 3p⁴, 3 is the shell, p is the subshell, and 4 is the number of electrons in that subshell.

First 20 neutral atoms: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s

This introductory ground-state filling guide has limits beyond the first 20 elements; some configurations are exceptions and ions require additional care.

5. Worked review: oxygen

Neutral oxygen has atomic number 8, so it has 8 electrons. Its ground-state configuration is:

1s2 2s2 2p4

The total is 2 + 2 + 4 = 8. Draw one box for 1s, one for 2s, and three for 2p:

↑↓
1s
↑↓
2s
↑↓↑↑
2p

Each box represents an orbital and each arrow an electron. Oxygen has two unpaired electrons in its ground-state 2p arrangement. Any one of the three equal-energy p boxes may be drawn as the paired box.

Rule How to check
Aufbau For the stated scope, use the introductory lower-energy ground-state filling sequence.
Pauli exclusion At most two electrons share an orbital, with opposite spins if paired.
Hund’s rule In a ground state, equal-energy orbitals are singly occupied with parallel spins before pairing.

An arrangement that is not the ground state is not automatically impossible: some arrangements can represent excited states.
