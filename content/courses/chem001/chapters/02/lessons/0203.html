Periodic Trends: Atomic Radius and Atomic Mass

Does an atom become larger as you move across a period? Does atomic mass follow the same direction? Use the periodic table to compare the two patterns.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Describe and diagram the general changes in atomic radius and relative atomic mass across a period and down a group.
  • Explain general atomic-radius trends using energy levels, shielding, and effective nuclear attraction.
  • Use periodic-table position and comparable data to justify simple comparisons.
  • Recognize that atomic-mass trends have exceptions and that atomic number determines table order.
Recall: A period is a horizontal row and a group is a vertical column. We will study electron energy levels in more detail in the next lesson.

2. Atomic radius: comparing atomic size

Atoms do not have sharply defined outer edges. Atomic radius is an estimate of atomic size based on a specified method—for example, a covalent radius is related to the distance between bonded atoms. Different definitions can give different values; compare data measured or defined consistently. Here we discuss neutral atoms.

Across a period: generally decreases left to right

Across a period, nuclear charge increases as the number of protons rises. Electrons are added to the same principal energy level, while shielding does not fully cancel the increasing nuclear charge. The stronger effective attraction generally pulls the outer electron distribution closer to the nucleus.

Example · Same period

Compare neutral sodium (Na) and chlorine (Cl), both in period 3. Na lies farther left. Using the general trend, Na has the larger atomic radius.

Down a group: generally increases

Going down a group adds occupied principal energy levels. The outer electrons generally occupy regions farther from the nucleus, and inner electrons provide additional shielding. The size increase generally outweighs the increased nuclear charge.

Example · Same group

Lithium (Li), sodium (Na), and potassium (K) are in group 1. The general order from smaller to larger neutral atomic radius is Li < Na < K.

Compare carefully: Radius definitions and available measurements vary, especially for different types of elements. A neutral atomic radius should not be compared directly with an ionic radius as if they were the same measurement. For a diagonal comparison, the across-period and down-group effects may compete; consult comparable data.

3. Relative atomic mass: a different pattern

For many elements, the value listed on the periodic table represents relative atomic mass, based on an abundance-weighted average of isotopic masses for a specified sample or reference composition. Some elements’ values may be given differently depending on the table and isotope availability. We will study isotopes and weighted averages in Chapter 3.

Relative atomic mass generally increases left to right across a period and down a group. As atomic number increases, atoms of successive elements generally have more protons and tend also to have more neutrons, although isotopic composition matters.

Comparison Approximate relative atomic masses Interpretation
Na and Cl, period 3 Na ≈ 22.99; Cl ≈ 35.45 Cl has greater relative atomic mass in this pair.
Li and Na, group 1 Li ≈ 6.94; Na ≈ 22.99 Na has greater relative atomic mass in this pair.
Te and I: an exception Te ≈ 127.60; I ≈ 126.90 Te precedes I by atomic number, yet its listed relative atomic mass is slightly greater.
Important: The periodic table is arranged by atomic number, not by increasing atomic mass. Relative atomic mass is dimensionless; the mass of an individual atom or isotope may be expressed in atomic mass units (u).

4. Compare the two trends

Direction Atomic radius Relative atomic mass
Left to right across a period Generally decreases Generally increases, with exceptions
Down a group Generally increases Generally increases

Try the reasoning

Na lies to the left of Cl in period 3. Na generally has the larger radius, but Cl has the greater relative atomic mass. A bigger atomic radius does not necessarily mean a greater atomic mass.

7. Key terms and takeaways

  • Atomic radius is an estimate of size; definitions and measurement methods matter.
  • Radius generally decreases left to right across a period and increases down a group.
  • Relative atomic mass generally increases across periods and down groups, with exceptions.
  • The periodic table is ordered by atomic number, the number of protons.
  • Compare elements in the same group or period first, and consult comparable data when trends are inconclusive.
