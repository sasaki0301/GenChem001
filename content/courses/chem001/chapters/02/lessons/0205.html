Electron Configuration: The Aufbau Principle

How can a short sequence such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ describe an atom’s electrons? Learn to read the notation and build ground-state configurations step by step.

Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Interpret the numbers, letters, and superscripts in electron-configuration notation.
  • Determine the electron count of a neutral atom from its atomic number.
  • Apply an introductory Aufbau filling sequence to write ground-state configurations for the first 20 elements.
  • Validate electron counts, allowed subshells, subshell capacities, and the introductory ground-state order.
Recall Lesson 4: The maximum subshell capacities are s², p⁶, d¹⁰, and f¹⁴. These are capacities, not the number of electrons every subshell must contain.

1. What does electron configuration show?

An electron configuration summarizes how an atom’s electrons are distributed among subshells. A ground-state configuration describes a lowest-energy arrangement of the atom, subject to quantum rules. An atom can also have excited states with different arrangements.

Read the term 3p4

Part Meaning
3 Principal energy level or shell, n = 3
p The p subshell within that shell
⁴ Four electrons in the 3p subshell

The superscript counts electrons in the whole subshell, not in one orbital. A p subshell has three orbitals.

For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the atomic number because the numbers of protons and electrons are equal. For example, neutral oxygen has atomic number 8 and therefore 8 electrons. Ions have different electron counts and are outside the main scope of this lesson.

2. The Aufbau principle

Aufbau means “building up.” As an introductory method for predicting many ground-state configurations, place electrons in lower-energy available orbitals before higher-energy ones, while respecting the rules that govern electron occupancy.

Filling guide for the first 20 neutral elements

1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s

Use the sequence above for the first 20 neutral elements, hydrogen through calcium. The 4s subshell comes after 3p in this introductory sequence; shell number alone does not always determine the filling order.

Subshell type Orbitals Maximum electrons
s 1 2
p 3 6
d 5 10
f 7 14
Scope and exceptions: For many introductory neutral-atom predictions, 4s is placed before 3d. Beyond the first 20 elements, subshell energies are close and can change with occupancy; some ground-state configurations, including chromium and copper, differ from the simplest filling prediction. Ion configurations require additional care.

The Pauli exclusion principle limits each orbital to two electrons with opposite spins. Hund’s rule describes how electrons occupy orbitals of equal energy before pairing. We will apply both to orbital-box diagrams in Lesson 6.

3. Write a ground-state configuration

  1. Identify the element’s atomic number and count electrons for the neutral atom.
  2. Follow the filling sequence and assign electrons without exceeding each subshell’s capacity.
  3. Stop when all electrons have been placed.
  4. Add the superscripts and check that the total matches the electron count.

Example 1 · Oxygen, atomic number 8

Neutral oxygen has 8 electrons. Fill 1s with 2 and 2s with 2; place the remaining 4 in 2p.

1s2 2s2 2p4

Check: 2 + 2 + 4 = 8 electrons. The 2p subshell is not full; it can hold up to 6.

Example 2 · Chlorine, atomic number 17

Neutral chlorine has 17 electrons. The first 10 occupy 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Add 2 in 3s, then place the remaining 5 in 3p.

1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p5

Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 5 = 17 electrons.

Example 3 · Calcium, atomic number 20

The first 18 electrons form the same configuration as argon. The remaining two occupy 4s in the introductory sequence.

1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2

Check: 2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 20 electrons.

Noble-gas shorthand

Use the preceding noble gas in brackets to represent its complete electron configuration, then add the remaining electrons. For calcium, [Ar] 4s2 represents 18 + 2 = 20 electrons. For chlorine, [Ne] 3s2 3p5 represents 10 + 2 + 5 = 17 electrons.

4. Check a proposed configuration

Check Ask yourself
Electron count Do the superscripts total the number of electrons in the stated atom?
Allowed labels Does the subshell exist? For example, 2p exists but 2d does not.
Capacity Does each subshell stay at or below its maximum, such as p⁶?
Ground-state sequence For the first 20 neutral elements, were lower-energy subshells filled in the introductory order?

Example · Identify the error

A student writes sodium (11 electrons) as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3p¹. The electron count is 11 and the capacities are allowed, but the order is wrong for sodium’s ground state: 3s comes before 3p. The corrected configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.

Having the correct total alone does not guarantee the ground-state arrangement. An arrangement that is not the ground state is not necessarily impossible; some arrangements may describe excited states.

7. Key terms and takeaways

  • Electron configuration: a summary of electrons distributed among subshells.
  • Neutral atom: electron count equals atomic number.
  • Superscript: the number of electrons in a subshell, not in a single orbital.
  • Aufbau guide for the first 20: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s.
  • Validation: check electron count, allowed subshells, capacity, and ground-state order.
