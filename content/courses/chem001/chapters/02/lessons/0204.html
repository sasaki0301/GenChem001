Energy Levels and Orbitals

How can an atom have organized electron energies when electrons do not travel in neat planetary tracks? The quantum model describes allowed energies and probabilities.

1. The quantum model

Atoms have allowed energy states. An electron in an atom can absorb energy and move to a higher allowed state; when it moves from a higher state to a lower one, energy can be emitted as a photon. The photon’s energy equals the difference between the two states:

Ephoton = Ehigher − Elower = hν

Here h is Planck’s constant and ν (nu) is the light frequency. We will explore the historical evidence for the quantum model in Chapter 3.

In the quantum model, an orbital is described by a wavefunction. Its probability distribution helps predict where an electron may be found. It is not a fixed circular path. An orbital diagram or shape is a representation, not a photograph of an electron moving along a line.

2. Principal energy levels: shells

A shell groups orbitals with the same principal quantum number n = 1, 2, 3, and so on. A larger n generally corresponds to a more extended electron distribution, although orbitals can overlap and do not form solid boundaries.

Maximum capacity of a shell

Maximum electrons in shell n = 2n²

Shell Allowed subshells Maximum electrons
n = 1 1s 2
n = 2 2s, 2p 8
n = 3 3s, 3p, 3d 18
n = 4 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f 32

For example, n = 3 gives 2(3)² = 18. These are maximum capacities; they do not say that every shell is full or specify the actual filling sequence.

3. Subshells and s, p, d, f notation

Each shell contains subshells, also called sublevels. In labels such as 3p, the number identifies the shell (n = 3) and the letter identifies the subshell (p).

How the categories fit together

Shell n = 3 → 3s, 3p, 3d subshells → orbitals in each subshell

For n = 1–4, the allowed labels are shown in the shell table above. A 2d subshell and a 3f subshell do not exist. For higher n, additional subshell types beyond f are possible; this introductory lesson focuses on s, p, d, and f.

Subshell type Orbitals per subshell Maximum electrons First allowed example
s 1 2 1s
p 3 6 2p
d 5 10 3d
f 7 14 4f

Each orbital can hold at most two electrons with opposite spins. Therefore, 3p has three orbitals and a maximum capacity of six electrons.

4. Orbitals: probability distributions, not paths

s orbital · schematic

An s orbital is spherically symmetric. A drawn boundary represents a chosen probability region, not a sharp edge.

p orbital · schematic

A p subshell contains three differently oriented p orbitals. The two lobes shown belong to one orbital, not two.

Many d and f orbital shapes are more complex. Their full geometry is beyond this introduction; focus on their orbital counts and capacities.

5. Draw discrete energy levels

Energy diagrams use separate lines for allowed states rather than a continuous ramp. The example below is a simplified hydrogen-atom model for n = 1–3; spacing is schematic and is not to scale.

The shared lines show equal energies within each n in this simple hydrogen model. The arrow illustrates absorption from n = 1 to n = 2; the photon energy matches the gap.

  1. Draw a vertical arrow pointing upward and label it energy.
  2. Draw separate horizontal lines for n = 1, 2, and 3. Label them 1s; 2s and 2p; and 3s, 3p, and 3d, respectively, for this simplified hydrogen model.
  3. Draw an upward arrow between two allowed levels for absorption, or a downward arrow for emission. Label the energy difference.
Do not overgeneralize: In multielectron atoms, subshells with the same n can have different energies, and subshells from different shells can overlap in energy. A diagram of shell categories is not automatically a universal filling order. We will learn an introductory filling sequence in Lesson 5.

8. Key terms and takeaways

  • Shell: orbitals sharing the same principal quantum number n.
  • Subshell: a grouping within a shell, labeled s, p, d, or f in this lesson.
  • Orbital: a quantum description associated with an electron probability distribution; not a fixed orbit.
  • Orbital counts: s = 1, p = 3, d = 5, f = 7; each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins.
  • Maximum shell capacity: 2n². Capacity and actual filling order are different ideas.
  • Energy transitions: absorption or emission corresponds to the difference between allowed states.
