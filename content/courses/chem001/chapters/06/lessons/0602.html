Writing Electron Configuration of Atoms

Real-World Scenario

Why is sodium highly reactive while neon is chemically stable? Why do metals conduct electricity while noble gases rarely react? The answers lie in how electrons are arranged around the nucleus. Electron configuration describes the distribution of electrons among energy levels, sublevels, and orbitals. Understanding electron configuration allows chemists to predict an element's chemical behavior, bonding patterns, and position in the periodic table.

Learning Objectives

  • Relate electron configuration to periodic table position.

What is Electron Configuration?

Electron configuration describes how electrons are distributed among the energy levels, sublevels, and orbitals of an atom.

Example

Hydrogen contains one electron.

1s¹

This means the single electron occupies the 1s orbital.

Electron configuration acts like an address showing where electrons are located within an atom.

The Aufbau Principle

The Aufbau Principle states that electrons occupy the lowest available energy orbitals before filling higher-energy orbitals.

Electrons fill orbitals from lowest energy to highest energy.

The filling order is determined experimentally and follows a predictable pattern.

The Pauli Exclusion Principle

Wolfgang Pauli proposed that no two electrons within an atom can possess the same set of four quantum numbers.

An orbital can contain at most two electrons, and those electrons must have opposite spins.

Hund's Rule states that electrons occupy orbitals singly before pairing.

Electrons spread out among equal-energy orbitals whenever possible.

Writing Electron Configurations Step-by-Step

To write an electron configuration:

  1. Determine the atomic number.
  2. Determine the total number of electrons.
  3. Fill orbitals according to the Aufbau Principle.
  4. Apply the Pauli Exclusion Principle.
  5. Apply Hund's Rule.

Example 1: Carbon

Carbon has atomic number 6.

Therefore, it contains 6 electrons.

Fill orbitals in order:

1s² 2s² 2p²

Example 3: Sodium

Sodium has atomic number 11.

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹

The outermost electron occupies the 3s orbital.

Example 4: Chlorine

Chlorine has atomic number 17.

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵

The valence shell contains seven electrons.

Noble Gas Shorthand Notation

For larger atoms, electron configurations can become lengthy.

Chemists simplify configurations using noble gas notation.

Sodium

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹
can be written as
[Ne] 3s¹

Calcium

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s²
becomes
[Ar] 4s²

Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons in the highest occupied principal energy level.

Valence electrons largely determine chemical properties and bonding behavior.

Example: Determining Valence Electrons

Magnesium:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²

Highest energy level = n = 3

Valence electrons = 2

Phosphorus:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³

Valence electrons = 5

Common Misconceptions

  • Electrons do not fill orbitals randomly.
  • Orbitals fill according to energy, not shell number alone.
  • Valence electrons are not always equal to total electrons.
  • Hund's Rule requires single occupation before pairing.
  • An orbital can hold only two electrons with opposite spins.

Lesson Summary

  • Electron configuration describes how electrons are distributed in an atom.
  • The Aufbau Principle governs orbital filling order.
  • The Pauli Exclusion Principle limits each orbital to two electrons with opposite spins.
  • Hund's Rule requires orbitals to fill singly before pairing.
  • Noble gas notation simplifies long electron configurations.
  • Valence electrons determine many chemical properties.
  • Electron configurations explain periodic trends and chemical behavior.
