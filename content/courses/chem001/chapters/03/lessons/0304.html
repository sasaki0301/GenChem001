Deriving the Chemical Formulas of Substances

Real-World Scenario

A laboratory analysis reveals that a compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Scientists must determine the compound's chemical formula before they can understand its properties or manufacture it. Chemical formulas are not guessed—they are derived from experimental data and the ratios of atoms present in a substance.

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate molecular formulas and empirical formulas.
  • Determine empirical formulas from atom ratios.
  • Determine empirical formulas from percent composition data.
  • Determine molecular formulas from empirical formulas.
  • Solve multi-step formula derivation problems.

Why Chemical Formulas Matter

A chemical formula tells us the types and numbers of atoms present in a substance. It serves as a shorthand representation of the composition of a compound.

Examples

  • H₂O → Water
  • CO₂ → Carbon Dioxide
  • C₆H₁₂O₆ → Glucose
  • NH₃ → Ammonia
Chemical formulas provide information about the composition of a substance and are essential for chemical calculations.

Empirical Formula vs Molecular Formula

Empirical Formula Molecular Formula
Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms Actual number of atoms in a molecule
CH₂O C₆H₁₂O₆
CH C₆H₆
HO H₂O₂
The empirical formula may be the same as the molecular formula, but not always.

Determining an Empirical Formula from Atom Ratios

If the number of atoms of each element is known, the empirical formula can be found by simplifying the ratio to the smallest whole numbers.

Worked Example 1

A compound contains:

  • 4 carbon atoms
  • 8 hydrogen atoms

Ratio:

C₄H₈

Divide each subscript by 4:

CH₂

Empirical Formula = CH₂

Determining Empirical Formula from Percent Composition

This is one of the most important applications of the mole concept.

To determine an empirical formula from percent composition:

1. Assume 100 g of the compound.
2. Convert grams to moles.
3. Divide by the smallest number of moles.
4. Convert to whole-number ratios.

Worked Example 2

A compound contains:

  • 40.0% Carbon
  • 6.7% Hydrogen
  • 53.3% Oxygen

Step 1: Assume 100 g sample

  • C = 40.0 g
  • H = 6.7 g
  • O = 53.3 g

Step 2: Convert grams to moles

C = 40.0 ÷ 12.01 = 3.33 mol
H = 6.7 ÷ 1.01 = 6.63 mol
O = 53.3 ÷ 16.00 = 3.33 mol

Step 3: Divide by smallest value (3.33)

C = 1
H = 2
O = 1

Step 4: Write formula

CH₂O

Empirical Formula = CH₂O

Determining a Molecular Formula

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

Molecular Formula = (Empirical Formula)n

To determine n:

n = Molecular Mass ÷ Empirical Formula Mass

Worked Example 3

A compound has:

  • Empirical Formula = CH₂O
  • Molar Mass = 180 g/mol

Step 1: Determine empirical formula mass

12.01 + (2 × 1.01) + 16.00 = 30.03 g/mol

Step 2: Calculate n

180 ÷ 30.03 ≈ 6

Step 3: Multiply subscripts by 6

(CH₂O)₆ = C₆H₁₂O₆

Molecular Formula = C₆H₁₂O₆

Handling Fractional Ratios

Sometimes dividing by the smallest mole value produces decimals instead of whole numbers.

Decimal Ratio Multiply By
0.5 2
0.33 3
0.25 4
0.67 3

Suppose the ratio obtained is:

C = 1
H = 1.5

Multiply all values by 2:

C₂H₃

Common Mistakes

  • Using atomic numbers instead of atomic masses.
  • Forgetting to convert percentages to grams by assuming a 100 g sample.
  • Failing to divide all mole values by the smallest value.
  • Rounding ratios too early.
  • Confusing empirical formulas with molecular formulas.

Check Your Understanding

1. What is the empirical formula of C₂H₄?

Divide subscripts by 2:
CH₂

2. What is the empirical formula of N₂O₄?

Divide subscripts by 2:
NO₂

3. A compound has an empirical formula mass of 44 g/mol and a molecular mass of 88 g/mol. What is n?

88 ÷ 44 = 2

4. If the empirical formula is CH₂ and n = 3, what is the molecular formula?

C₃H₆

5. Why do chemists assume a 100 g sample when solving percent composition problems?

Because percentages can be directly interpreted as grams, making calculations simpler.

Lesson Summary

  • Chemical formulas describe the composition of compounds.
  • The empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  • The molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms present.
  • Empirical formulas can be determined from atom ratios or percent composition data.
  • Molecular formulas are obtained using the empirical formula and molar mass.
  • Careful mole calculations are essential when deriving formulas.
