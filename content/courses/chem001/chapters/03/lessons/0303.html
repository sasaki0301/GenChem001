Isotopes and Atomic Identity

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon, even though their mass numbers differ. What makes them the same element, and what makes them different isotopes?

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Identify protons, neutrons, and electrons by charge and location.
  • Use atomic number and mass number to determine proton and neutron counts.
  • Interpret isotope notation and names and explain what makes atoms isotopes of an element.
  • Determine electron counts for neutral atoms and simple ions.
  • Distinguish an isotope’s mass number from the relative atomic mass listed on a periodic table.
Connection: In Lessons 1 and 2 we traced how models of the atom developed. We now use the nuclear model to describe atomic identity. Radioisotopes and abundance-weighted average atomic mass are studied later.

1. The particles inside an atom

Particle Relative charge Location Why it matters here
Proton (p⁺) +1 Nucleus Determines the element.
Neutron (n) 0 Nucleus Different numbers give isotopes of the same element.
Electron (e⁻) −1 Outside the nucleus, described by quantum states Electron gain or loss changes an atom’s charge.

Atomic number (Z) is the number of protons. Every atom with Z = 6 is carbon. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.

Mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of a specified atom.

A = protons + neutrons   ·   neutrons = A − Z

Mass number is a count and is always a whole number. It is not the mass of the nucleus in grams and is not the same thing as the relative atomic mass printed on many periodic tables.

2. Read isotope notation

The example below represents carbon-14. The left superscript is the mass number A, the left subscript is atomic number Z, and the center is the element symbol.

146C

Part Meaning For carbon-14
Symbol C Element symbol Carbon
Atomic number, lower left Proton count Z 6 protons
Mass number, upper left Protons + neutrons A 14 nucleons

Neutrons = 14 − 6 = 8. No charge is shown, so this notation represents a neutral atom: 6 electrons.

Worked example · Sodium-23

Sodium has Z = 11. The notation 2311Na gives A = 23 and Z = 11. Therefore, the neutral atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons, and 11 electrons.

The hyphenated name sodium-23 also identifies the mass number. Find Z from the periodic table if it is not written.

3. Same element, different isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same atomic number and different mass numbers.

Neutral atom Protons Neutrons Electrons Mass number
Carbon-12 6 6 6 12
Carbon-13 6 7 6 13
Carbon-14 6 8 6 14

Each has six protons, so each is carbon. The neutron count distinguishes these isotopes. Some isotopes are stable and others radioactive; we will study radioactive decay and applications in a later lesson.

Do not confuse the terms: A change in proton count gives a different element. A change in neutron count gives a different isotope of the same element. A change in electron count changes the charge and produces an ion.

4. Count electrons in ions

An ion is an atom or group with a net electric charge. This lesson considers single-atom ions. The charge is the number of protons minus the number of electrons.

charge = protons − electrons   ·   electrons = Z − charge

Use the signed charge in the formula: +1 for Na⁺ and −1 for Cl⁻. A positive ion has lost electrons; a negative ion has gained electrons. In ion formation, the proton and neutron counts in the nucleus remain unchanged.

Worked example · Sodium-23 ion

For 2311Na+: protons = 11; neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12; electrons = 11 − (+1) = 10. The +1 charge means one electron was removed from a neutral sodium atom.

Worked example · Chloride-35 ion

For 3517Cl−: protons = 17; neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18; electrons = 17 − (−1) = 18. The −1 charge means one extra electron compared with neutral chlorine.

5. Mass number and relative atomic mass

Carbon-12 has mass number 12: it counts the 6 protons and 6 neutrons in that nuclide. A periodic table may list a decimal relative atomic mass for carbon, reflecting the weighted contributions of isotopes in a specified natural sample or reference composition. The two values answer different questions.

Do not infer the exact neutron count of a particular atom by rounding a periodic-table average unless the isotope is specified. We will calculate abundance-weighted averages in a later lesson.

Challenge · Compare two particles

Particle A is neutral carbon-14. Particle B is carbon-14 with a 2− charge. Compare their proton, neutron, and electron counts.

Show one possible answer

Both have 6 protons and 8 neutrons, so both represent carbon-14. Neutral A has 6 electrons; B has 8 electrons because its charge is 2−. They are the same isotope in different charge states.

8. Key terms and takeaways

  • Atomic number Z: protons; determines the element.
  • Mass number A: protons plus neutrons; neutron count is A − Z.
  • Isotopes: same proton count, different neutron counts.
  • Neutral atom: electrons equal protons. For ions, electrons = Z − signed charge.
  • Relative atomic mass: a different quantity from the mass number of an individual nuclide.
