Atomic Identity: Protons, Neutrons, and Electrons

What makes a carbon atom carbon? Explore the particles inside atoms and use simple relationships to determine their numbers.

ObjectivesParticlesAtomic identityMass numberElectronsPracticeTakeaways

Learning objectives

At the end of the lesson, you should be able to:

  • Describe the charges and locations of protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Use atomic number to identify an element and determine its number of protons.
  • Calculate mass number or neutron count using proton and neutron counts.
  • Determine the number of electrons in neutral atoms and simple monatomic ions.
Connect: Earlier lessons showed how evidence led to the nuclear model. Now use that model to describe individual atoms. In Lesson 4, you will use these skills to compare isotopes and write isotope notation.

1. Meet the subatomic particles

An atom has a tiny nucleus containing protons and neutrons. Electrons occupy states around the nucleus; they are described by regions of probability rather than fixed planetary paths.

Schematic, not to scale. The dashed outline is not an electron orbit.

Particle Relative charge Location Role in this lesson
Proton (p⁺) +1 Nucleus Determines element identity.
Neutron (n) 0 Nucleus Contributes to mass number.
Electron (e⁻) −1 Outside the nucleus Affects the atom’s net charge.

2. Atomic number determines identity

The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons. It identifies the element. Every carbon atom has 6 protons, and every oxygen atom has 8.

atomic number Z = number of protons

Worked example · Identify an element

An atom has 12 protons and 13 neutrons. Its atomic number is 12. Find Z = 12 in a periodic table: the element is magnesium (Mg). The neutron count does not change the element’s identity.

Think it through

One atom has 8 protons, another has 9. Can both be oxygen?

Show answer

No. An atom with 8 protons is oxygen; an atom with 9 protons is fluorine. Different proton counts mean different elements.

3. Mass number and neutron count

The mass number (A) counts the protons and neutrons in a particular atom. Electrons are not included in this count.

mass number A = protons + neutrons
neutrons = A − Z

Worked example · Find mass number

A neutral nitrogen atom has 7 protons and 8 neutrons. A = 7 + 8 = 15. Because Z = 7, the atom is nitrogen. Its mass number is 15.

Worked example · Find neutrons

A sodium atom has atomic number 11 and mass number 23. It has 11 protons and 23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

Mass number is a whole-number count for a specified atom. It is different from the decimal relative atomic mass often shown on the periodic table. Do not infer a particular atom’s neutron count from a rounded average unless the atom’s mass number is specified.

4. Count electrons and interpret charge

In a neutral atom, positive proton charge balances negative electron charge, so electrons = protons = Z. If an atom loses or gains electrons, it becomes an ion. In this section, consider only ions made from one atom.

charge = protons − electrons
electrons = Z − signed charge

Particle Protons Electrons Reason
Neutral sodium, Na 11 11 Equal charges balance.
Sodium ion, Na⁺ 11 10 One electron was lost.
Chloride ion, Cl⁻ 17 18 One electron was gained.

Worked example · A 2+ ion

Magnesium has Z = 12. For Mg²⁺, electrons = 12 − (+2) = 10. It still has 12 protons; forming this ion does not change its nucleus.

Keep the changes separate: Proton count determines the element; changing the neutron count changes its mass number; changing the electron count changes its charge.

Challenge · Explain your reasoning

A neutral atom has 16 protons and 18 neutrons. Identify its element, mass number, and electron count.

Show a possible answer

Z = 16 is sulfur. A = 16 + 18 = 34. Because the atom is neutral, it has 16 electrons.

6. Key terms and takeaways

  • Protons, neutrons, and electrons have charges +1, 0, and −1, respectively.
  • Atomic number Z equals the proton count and identifies the element.
  • Mass number A = protons + neutrons; neutrons = A − Z.
  • Neutral atoms have equal proton and electron counts; ions have unequal counts.
