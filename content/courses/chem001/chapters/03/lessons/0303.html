Counting Atoms and Molecules

Real-World Scenario

A pharmaceutical company produces billions of molecules of a medicine in a single tablet. Scientists cannot count these molecules individually, yet they must know exactly how many particles are present to ensure the medicine works properly. Chemists use the mole concept and Avogadro's number to determine the number of atoms, molecules, and formula units in a sample.

Learning Objectives

  • Convert between moles and particles.
  • Determine the number of atoms, molecules, and formula units in a sample.
  • Interpret chemical formulas when counting atoms.
  • Calculate the total number of atoms in compounds.
  • Solve multi-step particle-counting problems.

Particles in Chemistry

The term particle refers to the smallest representative unit of a substance.

Type of Substance Particle Counted
Element (Fe, Cu, Au) Atoms
Molecular Compound (H₂O, CO₂) Molecules
Ionic Compound (NaCl, CaCl₂) Formula Units
Regardless of the type of particle, one mole always contains:

6.022 × 10²³ particles

Converting Moles to Particles

To determine the number of particles in a sample, multiply the number of moles by Avogadro's number.

Number of Particles = Moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Worked Example 1

How many molecules are present in 2.50 mol of CO₂?

Step 1: Write the conversion factor.

1 mol = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules

Step 2: Multiply.

2.50 × 6.022 × 10²³

Answer:

1.51 × 10²⁴ molecules

Converting Particles to Moles

To convert particles back to moles, divide by Avogadro's number.

Moles = Particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Worked Example 2

How many moles are represented by 3.011 × 10²³ molecules of NH₃?

Step 1: Apply the formula.

3.011 × 10²³ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Answer:

0.500 mol NH₃

Counting Atoms in Chemical Formulas

Chemical formulas tell us how many atoms of each element are present in one molecule or formula unit.

Formula Atoms Present Total Atoms
H₂O 2 H + 1 O 3
CO₂ 1 C + 2 O 3
NH₃ 1 N + 3 H 4
C₆H₁₂O₆ 6 C + 12 H + 6 O 24
Subscripts indicate the number of atoms of each element present in one molecule.

Counting Total Atoms from Moles

Sometimes we need to determine the total number of atoms, not just molecules.

Worked Example 3

How many oxygen atoms are present in 2.00 mol of H₂O?

Step 1: Calculate molecules.

2.00 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

Step 2: Determine atoms per molecule.

Each H₂O molecule contains 1 oxygen atom.

Answer:

1.204 × 10²⁴ oxygen atoms

Counting Atoms in Compounds

Worked Example 4

How many hydrogen atoms are present in 1.00 mol of H₂O?

Step 1: Calculate molecules.

1.00 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules

Step 2: Each water molecule contains 2 hydrogen atoms.

2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ hydrogen atoms

Answer:

1.204 × 10²⁴ hydrogen atoms

Particle Counting Strategy

When solving particle-counting problems:

1. Identify what is being counted.
2. Convert between moles and particles using Avogadro's number.
3. Use subscripts to determine atoms per molecule.
4. Multiply by the number of atoms required.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing molecules with atoms.
  • Ignoring subscripts in chemical formulas.
  • Forgetting that ionic compounds contain formula units instead of molecules.
  • Using multiplication instead of division when converting particles to moles.
  • Not accounting for multiple atoms of an element within a molecule.

Check Your Understanding

1. How many molecules are present in 0.250 mol of CO₂?

0.250 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.51 × 10²³ molecules

2. How many atoms are present in one molecule of CH₄?

1 carbon atom + 4 hydrogen atoms
5 total atoms

3. How many oxygen atoms are present in one molecule of CO₂?

Each molecule contains:
2 oxygen atoms

4. How many formula units are present in 3.00 mol of NaCl?

3.00 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.807 × 10²⁴ formula units

5. How many total atoms are present in one molecule of C₂H₆?

2 carbon atoms + 6 hydrogen atoms
8 total atoms

Lesson Summary

  • One mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles.
  • Particles may be atoms, molecules, or formula units depending on the substance.
  • Moles and particles can be converted using Avogadro's number.
  • Chemical formulas indicate how many atoms are present in a molecule or formula unit.
  • Subscripts must be considered when counting atoms.
  • Particle-counting problems often require multiple conversion steps.
