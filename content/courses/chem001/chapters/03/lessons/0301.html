Early Atomic Models: From Dalton to Rutherford

What could scientists learn about something they could not see directly? Follow the evidence that changed the atom from an indivisible particle into a model with electrons and a nucleus.

1. Three models at a glance

Each sketch represents an idea, not an image of an actual atom. The drawings are schematic and are not to scale.

2. Dalton: atoms in chemical reactions

In the early nineteenth century, John Dalton developed an atomic theory to explain patterns in chemical composition. He proposed that elements consist of atoms and that compounds form when atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios. His model treated the atom as indivisible in ordinary chemical changes.

Connect a model to evidence

A pure compound has a consistent composition. Dalton’s particle model helped explain why a compound can be described by a definite ratio of atoms. The model supplied a way to reason about reactions even before the atom’s internal components were known.

Historical claim and current view: Dalton treated atoms of a given element as alike and indivisible. Later evidence revealed subatomic particles and isotopes. Atoms are rearranged in ordinary chemical reactions, but nuclear processes can change nuclei.

3. Thomson: evidence for electrons

In experiments with cathode rays in evacuated tubes, J. J. Thomson studied how the rays responded to electric and magnetic fields. Their deflection supported the conclusion that the beam contained negatively charged particles. He inferred a charge-to-mass ratio for these particles, now called electrons. Similar behavior from different cathode materials supported the idea that electrons are components of many kinds of matter.

Observation → inference → model

Observation: Cathode rays deflect in an electric field toward a positive plate.

Inference: The beam includes negatively charged particles.

Model: Thomson proposed electrons embedded in a distributed positive region, accounting for an overall neutral atom. The model is often called the plum-pudding model.

Thomson’s work challenged the claim that atoms are indivisible. His model did not contain the compact nucleus later supported by scattering evidence.

4. Rutherford: evidence for a nucleus

In the experiments conducted by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden under Ernest Rutherford’s direction, positively charged alpha particles were directed at very thin metal foil. A detector registered where particles arrived. Most passed through with little deflection; a small fraction were deflected through large angles, and very few scattered backward.

Read the evidence

Observation Inference in the nuclear model
Most alpha particles passed through or deflected only slightly. The dense nucleus occupies only a tiny fraction of the atom’s volume; most of the volume is comparatively empty.
A small fraction deflected through large angles. Positive charge and much of the mass are concentrated in a small region that can strongly repel a nearby positive alpha particle.

Rutherford proposed an atom with a small, dense, positively charged nucleus and electrons outside it. The phrase “mostly empty space” describes the tiny relative size of the nucleus; it does not mean electrons are absent from the surrounding region.

What remained unexplained?

The nuclear model did not adequately explain why atoms are stable or the observed pattern of atomic light spectra. Those questions helped motivate later models of electron energies, which we will examine in Lesson 2.

5. Compare the changing models

Model Core contribution Evidence or limitation to consider
Dalton Atoms and whole-number combinations helped explain composition. Its original indivisibility claim was challenged by subatomic particles.
Thomson Included electrons within a positively charged atom. Diffuse positive charge did not account well for large-angle alpha scattering.
Rutherford Concentrated positive charge and much of the mass in a nucleus. Did not adequately explain atomic stability or spectral patterns.

Try an explanation

A classmate says, “Most alpha particles passed through, so atoms must contain no particles at all.” Identify the error and use both scattering observations in your answer.

Show a possible response

Passing through suggests the compact nucleus occupies little of the atom’s volume, not that the atom contains no particles. The rare large deflections provide evidence for a concentrated positive nucleus; electrons are also part of the atom.

8. Key takeaways

  • Scientific models change when new evidence requires a better explanation.
  • Dalton used atoms to explain chemical composition; his indivisibility claim was later revised.
  • Cathode-ray studies supported electrons and Thomson’s model of distributed positive charge.
  • Alpha-particle scattering supported Rutherford’s small, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Later models addressed unresolved questions about electrons, energies, and atomic stability.
