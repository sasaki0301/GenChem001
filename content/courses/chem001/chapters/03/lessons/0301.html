The Mole Concept and Avogadro’s Number

Learning Objectives

  • Explain why chemists use the mole as a counting unit
  • Define Avogadro’s number
  • Convert between moles and particles
  • Interpret the mole as a bridge between microscopic particles and measurable amounts

1. Why Do Chemists Need the Mole?

Atoms, ions, and molecules are extremely small. Because they are too tiny to count one by one in ordinary laboratory work, chemists use a special counting unit called the mole.

A mole works like other counting units. For example, a dozen means 12 items, a pair means 2 items, and a mole means a specific number of particles.

Key Idea: The mole is a counting unit used to represent a very large number of atoms, molecules, ions, or formula units.

Comparison

  • 1 dozen = 12 objects
  • 1 pair = 2 objects
  • 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles

Why is it impractical to count atoms one by one?

Atoms are incredibly small and exist in enormous numbers, so direct counting is impossible in ordinary laboratory measurements.

2. Avogadro’s Number

Avogadro’s number is the number of particles found in exactly one mole of a substance.

Avogadro’s Number: 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles

The word particles can refer to different things depending on the substance:

  • For elements such as Fe, particles are atoms
  • For molecular substances such as H₂O, particles are molecules
  • For ionic compounds such as NaCl, particles are formula units

The mole connects a measurable amount of substance to a very large number of particles.

In 1 mole of water, what kind of particles are being counted?

Molecules. One mole of water contains 6.022 × 10²³ water molecules.

3. Converting Moles to Particles

To convert from moles to particles, multiply by Avogadro’s number.

Formula

Number of particles = moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Example 1

How many atoms are in 2.00 moles of carbon?

Step 1: Identify the conversion factor:
1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ atoms

Step 2: Multiply:
2.00 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ atoms/mol

Answer:
1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms of carbon

How many molecules are in 0.50 mole of O₂?

0.50 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.011 × 10²³ molecules of O₂

4. Converting Particles to Moles

To convert from particles to moles, divide by Avogadro’s number.

Formula

Moles = number of particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Example 2

How many moles are represented by 3.011 × 10²³ molecules of CO₂?

Step 1: Use Avogadro’s number:
1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules

Step 2: Divide:
3.011 × 10²³ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Answer:
0.500 mole of CO₂

How many moles are in 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms of sodium?

1.204 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.00 moles of sodium atoms

5. Understanding What the Mole Really Means

The mole does not tell us what kind of substance we have. It only tells us how many particles are present. One mole of carbon atoms and one mole of water molecules contain the same number of particles, but they are different substances and have different masses.

Key Idea: Equal moles contain equal numbers of particles, but not necessarily equal masses.

Example

  • 1 mole of carbon atoms = 6.022 × 10²³ carbon atoms
  • 1 mole of water molecules = 6.022 × 10²³ water molecules
  • But their masses are different because carbon atoms and water molecules have different masses
Common Mistake: Do not assume that 1 mole of every substance has the same mass. The number of particles is the same, but the mass depends on the substance.

Practice Drills

Convert 3.00 moles of helium atoms to atoms.

3.00 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.807 × 10²⁴ atoms

Convert 6.022 × 10²³ molecules of NH₃ to moles.

6.022 × 10²³ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.00 mole of NH₃

Convert 1.50 moles of NaCl to formula units.

1.50 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 9.033 × 10²³ formula units of NaCl

Checkpoint Challenge

A sample contains 2.409 × 10²⁴ molecules of H₂O.
How many moles of H₂O are present?

2.409 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ = 4.00 moles of H₂O

Which contains more particles: 1 mole of oxygen gas or 1 mole of carbon dioxide?

They contain the same number of particles: 6.022 × 10²³ particles. One mole always contains Avogadro’s number of particles.

Key Terms

  • Mole: A counting unit equal to 6.022 × 10²³ particles
  • Avogadro’s Number: 6.022 × 10²³ particles per mole
  • Particle: The item being counted, such as an atom, molecule, ion, or formula unit
  • Formula Unit: The simplest ratio of ions in an ionic compound
