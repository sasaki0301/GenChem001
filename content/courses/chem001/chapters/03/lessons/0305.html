Radioisotopes: Sources and Types of Radiation

Some atomic nuclei change spontaneously and emit radiation. Where do these radioisotopes come from, and how do alpha, beta, and gamma radiation differ?

ObjectivesRadioisotopesSourcesTypes of radiationNuclear changesPractice

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain radioisotopes, radioactive decay, and ionizing radiation.
  • Identify examples of naturally occurring and human-produced radioisotope sources.
  • Compare alpha, beta, and gamma radiation by composition, charge, and typical penetration.
  • Interpret simple alpha and beta-minus nuclear equations using mass number and atomic number.
  • Explain why radiation risk depends on the exposure and where the radioactive material is located.
Connection to Lesson 4: Isotopes of an element share the same number of protons but have different numbers of neutrons. Some isotopes are stable; others have unstable nuclei and are radioactive.

1. What is a radioisotope?

A radioisotope is an isotope with an unstable nucleus that can change spontaneously through radioactive decay. The original nucleus is the parent; the product nucleus is the daughter. A daughter may be stable or may decay again.

Decay can emit particles or electromagnetic radiation, often carrying energy away from the nucleus. Alpha particles, beta particles, and gamma rays are forms of ionizing radiation: they can remove electrons from atoms or molecules directly or indirectly. This can alter molecules, including those in living tissue.

From isotope to radioisotope

Carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have 6 protons. Carbon-12 is stable, while carbon-14 is radioactive and undergoes beta-minus decay. Radioactivity is a property of a particular nucleus, not a reason to call every isotope radioactive.

Random and predictable: We cannot predict exactly when one nucleus will decay. For a large collection, however, the fraction expected to decay over a given time is predictable. Lesson 6 explores this pattern using half-life.

2. Natural and human-produced sources

Radioisotopes occur naturally and can also be produced by nuclear reactions. The classification describes how a particular sample originated; the same nuclide can sometimes be produced by both natural and human processes.

Source or production Examples How it occurs or is produced
Natural Uranium-238; radon-222 Uranium occurs in rocks and soils; radon-222 forms in a natural decay chain and can enter air.
Natural Carbon-14 Forms in the atmosphere through reactions initiated by cosmic radiation and enters the carbon cycle.
Human-produced Cobalt-60 Made by neutron activation in a nuclear reactor for uses including radiotherapy and sterilization.
Human-produced Iodine-131; cesium-137 Can be produced through nuclear fission in reactors; iodine-131 has medical applications.

Apply the idea

A laboratory produces a radioactive sample of a nuclide also found in nature. How should that sample be classified?

Show answer

The particular laboratory sample is human-produced. A different sample of the same nuclide may have a natural origin. Its nuclear properties do not depend on its origin.

3. Alpha, beta, and gamma radiation

The penetration comparisons below are general patterns. Actual shielding depends on radiation energy, material, thickness, and source geometry.

Type What it is Charge Typical penetration and shielding
Alpha (α) 2 protons and 2 neutrons: a helium-4 nucleus +2 Short range; a sheet of paper or outer dead layer of skin generally stops external alpha particles.
Beta-minus (β⁻) A high-speed electron emitted during nuclear decay −1 Often passes through paper; plastic or a thin sheet of metal can reduce or stop many beta particles depending on their energy.
Gamma (γ) A high-energy electromagnetic photon, not a material particle with rest mass 0 Generally more penetrating; dense lead or thick concrete attenuates gamma radiation. The amount transmitted depends on thickness and energy.

There is also beta-plus (β⁺) radiation, consisting of positrons. Here the worked nuclear example uses beta-minus. A radioactive nucleus can emit gamma radiation after another decay event; some nuclides emit more than one kind of radiation.

Conceptual comparison, not to scale. Gamma rays are attenuated rather than guaranteed to be completely blocked.

Inside versus outside: Alpha radiation has low external penetration, but alpha-emitting material can be particularly harmful if inhaled or swallowed. Beta and gamma exposure can also damage tissue. Do not infer safety from penetration alone.

4. Follow changes in the nucleus

In a nuclear equation, the total mass number A and atomic number Z balance across the arrow. These conservation checks help identify the daughter nucleus. The beta-minus example includes an antineutrino, which carries no electric charge or nucleon number.

Alpha decay

23892U → 23490Th + 42He (α)

A decreases by 4 and Z decreases by 2 in the daughter: 238 = 234 + 4 and 92 = 90 + 2.

Beta-minus decay

146C → 147N + 0−1e (β⁻) + antineutrino

A neutron changes into a proton, producing an electron and an antineutrino. The daughter’s A stays 14, while Z increases from 6 to 7. Check: 6 = 7 + (−1); the antineutrino has A = 0 and Z = 0.

Gamma emission generally releases excess nuclear energy without changing A or Z. Gamma rays are not alpha or beta particles.

Challenge · Apply your understanding

A nuclide has A = 210 and Z = 84. It emits an alpha particle. Give the daughter’s mass number and atomic number.

Show answer

A = 210 − 4 = 206; Z = 84 − 2 = 82. Atomic number 82 is lead, so the daughter is lead-206.

6. Key takeaways

  • Radioisotopes have unstable nuclei; a sample may be natural or human-produced.
  • Alpha particles are helium nuclei, beta-minus particles are electrons, and gamma rays are photons.
  • Penetration and shielding vary; internal alpha contamination can be dangerous.
  • Alpha emission changes A and Z; beta-minus emission raises Z while A stays the same.
