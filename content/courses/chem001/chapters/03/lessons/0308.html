1. How models of the atom developed

Model or idea Contribution and evidence Limit or later development
Dalton Atoms helped explain fixed ratios in compounds and chemical reactions. His indivisible-atom idea was revised after subatomic particles were discovered.
Thomson Cathode-ray evidence supported negatively charged electrons. His model placed electrons within a diffuse positive region. It did not account for the concentrated nucleus suggested by scattering.
Rutherford Most alpha particles passed through foil; rare large deflections supported a small, dense, positively charged nucleus. It did not adequately explain atomic stability and line spectra.
Bohr Electrons in hydrogen were assigned specific energy levels. Transitions explain hydrogen spectral lines. It works well for hydrogen-like systems but does not fully explain many-electron atoms.
Quantum model Electrons are described using quantum states and orbitals: probability distributions rather than precise circular paths. Models describe evidence and can be refined as new evidence appears.

Energy and light: An electron absorbs energy to move to a higher permitted level; a transition to a lower level can emit a photon with energy equal to the difference between levels, ΔE = hν. Higher photon frequency corresponds to greater photon energy.

Explain the evidence

Why did rare large-angle alpha-particle deflections suggest a concentrated positive nucleus?

Show a possible answer

A small, concentrated positive region can strongly repel a positively charged alpha particle passing close to it. The fact that most particles passed through suggests that the nucleus occupies only a tiny fraction of the atom’s volume.

2. Atomic identity and particle counts

Particle Charge Location Key idea
Proton +1 Nucleus Proton count determines element identity.
Neutron 0 Nucleus Different neutron counts produce isotopes of the same element.
Electron −1 Outside the nucleus Gain or loss changes an atom’s charge.

Z = protons   |   A = protons + neutrons   |   neutrons = A − Z

For a neutral atom, electrons = protons. For a monatomic ion, electrons = Z − signed charge. Insert +1 for a 1+ ion and −1 for a 1− ion.

Worked example · Chloride-35

For 3517Cl−: protons = 17; neutrons = 35 − 17 = 18; electrons = 17 − (−1) = 18. It remains chlorine because it still has 17 protons.

3. Isotopes and isotope notation

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton count but different neutron counts. In isotope notation, write mass number A at the upper left and atomic number Z at the lower left of the element symbol. A charge, if shown, appears at the upper right.

Neutral isotope Protons Neutrons Electrons
Carbon-12 6 6 6
Carbon-14 6 8 6

In the hyphenated name carbon-14, 14 is the mass number. Changing neutrons changes the isotope; changing electrons makes an ion; changing protons changes the element. Some isotopes are stable and some are radioactive.

4. Radioisotopes and radiation

A radioisotope has an unstable nucleus that can decay spontaneously. Natural sources include uranium in rocks, radon from decay chains, and carbon-14 formed in the atmosphere. Radioisotopes can also be produced by nuclear reactions; cobalt-60 and iodine-131 are examples of commonly human-produced radioisotopes. Source classification describes the sample’s origin.

Radiation Nature and charge Typical penetration Key nuclear change
Alpha (α) 2 protons and 2 neutrons; +2 Short range; paper or outer skin generally stops external alpha particles. Daughter A decreases by 4 and Z by 2.
Beta-minus (β⁻) Electron emitted in nuclear decay; −1 Often passes through paper; plastic or thin metal can stop many beta particles depending on energy. A remains the same; Z increases by 1.
Gamma (γ) High-energy electromagnetic photon; no charge Generally more penetrating; dense shielding attenuates it. Gamma emission alone does not change A or Z.
Penetration and shielding depend on radiation energy and material. Alpha-emitting material inside the body can be hazardous despite alpha’s low external penetration. Some nuclides emit more than one type of radiation.

Worked example · Alpha decay

23892U → 23490Th + 42He. Check the mass numbers: 238 = 234 + 4. Check atomic numbers: 92 = 90 + 2.

5. Half-life, applications, and risks

Physical half-life is the time required for half the radioactive parent nuclei in a large sample to decay. The exact time at which one nucleus decays is random, while large samples follow a predictable statistical pattern.

n = elapsed time ÷ half-life
parent amount remaining = initial amount × (1/2)n

Worked example · Half-life

A 120 mg parent sample has a half-life of 4 hours. After 12 hours, n = 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Repeated halving gives 120 → 60 → 30 → 15 mg. The remaining fraction is 12.5%, so 87.5% of the original parent nuclei have decayed. Daughter products may remain or be radioactive.

Medical applications: Technetium-99m is used in diagnostic imaging, iodine-131 in some thyroid treatments, and sealed cobalt-60 sources in external radiotherapy. The benefit, dose, radiation type, biological uptake, and elimination all matter; a short half-life alone does not guarantee safety.

Ionizing radiation can damage cells and DNA. Risk depends on dose, radiation type, exposed tissue, and whether material is inside or outside the body. Time, distance, suitable shielding, and preventing contamination are important protection principles. Medical procedures and source handling require trained professionals.

6. Relative and average atomic mass

Mass number is a whole-number count of protons and neutrons for a specific nuclide. Isotopic mass is an isotope’s measured mass, commonly in u. Average atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average for a specified mixture.

One unified atomic mass unit (1 u) is one-twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Relative atomic mass is the ratio of the average atomic mass to 1 u and is dimensionless; its numerical value matches the average mass expressed in u for the same mixture.

average atomic mass = Σ (isotope mass × fractional abundance)

Worked example · Weighted average

A sample has isotopes of 24.00 u at 70.0% and 26.00 u at 30.0%. Convert percentages to fractions: 0.700 and 0.300.

(24.00 × 0.700) + (26.00 × 0.300) = 24.6 u. For this mixture the relative atomic mass is 24.6 without a unit.

Check: the answer is between 24.00 and 26.00 and closer to the more abundant isotope. Isotopic mass is not exactly the same as mass number; using whole-number mass numbers gives an estimate.

Isotopic abundances can vary among samples; some standard atomic weights are expressed as intervals. Use the abundance data provided for the sample in a calculation.

Challenge · Connect the chapter

A neutral sample of a fictional element contains an isotope with 12 protons and 13 neutrons. It makes up 25.0% of the sample and has an isotopic mass of 25.0 u. The remaining 75.0% has mass 24.0 u. Identify the element and mass number of the first isotope, then calculate the sample’s average atomic mass.

Show a possible answer

Z = 12 identifies magnesium; A = 12 + 13 = 25, so the first isotope is magnesium-25. Average = (25.0 × 0.250) + (24.0 × 0.750) = 24.25 u, or about 24.3 u to three significant figures. The unrounded value lies closer to 24.0 u because that isotope is more abundant.
