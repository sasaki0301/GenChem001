1. Planck and energy quanta

In 1900 Max Planck developed a description of radiation emitted by heated objects that matched observations when energy exchange was restricted to discrete amounts. His treatment used energies in whole-number multiples of hν, where ν is frequency and h is Planck’s constant. This was a key step toward quantum theory.

For a photon of frequency ν, its energy is:

E = hν

Here h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and frequency ν is measured in hertz (Hz), or s⁻¹. At the same frequency, each photon has energy hν. Later work, including Einstein’s light-quantum explanation of the photoelectric effect, developed the photon idea; Planck’s original work addressed thermal radiation.

Worked example · Energy of one photon

For ν = 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz, calculate E using h ≈ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

E = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)(6.0 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹) ≈ 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

The units of seconds cancel. The final value is shown to two significant figures, following the frequency given.

2. Bohr and the hydrogen atom

In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed a model for hydrogen in which the electron could occupy certain allowed orbits, each with a specific energy. In that model, an electron in an allowed stationary state does not continuously radiate energy; energy changes when it moves between allowed states.

Bohr’s model explained the wavelengths of hydrogen’s spectral lines particularly well. It did not fully explain the spectra and behavior of multielectron atoms. Its fixed circular paths were later replaced by quantum-mechanical descriptions.

Evidence and interpretation

Observation: Excited hydrogen emits light at a set of distinct wavelengths.

Model: Allowed energy states have specific energy differences, so transitions can produce photons with specific energies.

Scope: The model is especially successful for hydrogen and hydrogen-like one-electron systems; the full quantum model provides a broader framework.

3. Read an energy-level diagram

Energy increases upward. Separate lines represent allowed states; the vertical spacing shown below is schematic and not to scale.

Absorption

The atom can absorb a photon with energy matching an allowed gap, moving from the lower to the higher state.

Emission

A transition from higher to lower can emit a photon whose energy equals that gap.

For transitions between the same two states, the energy gap is the same in either direction, but absorption and emission are different processes. A larger gap corresponds to higher photon frequency, because E = hν.

4. Schrödinger and the quantum model

Erwin Schrödinger developed a wave equation that can describe electron states in atoms. Its solutions, called wavefunctions, are used to calculate probability distributions. An orbital is associated with such a wavefunction; it does not specify a fixed planetary path for an electron.

Orbitals and their labels, such as 1s and 2p, connect this historical development to Chapter 2. A drawn orbital boundary represents a selected probability region, not a hard edge or an electron’s actual route.

Feature Bohr’s model Quantum model
Electron description Electron in specified orbits for the model. Wavefunction and probability distribution; no definite fixed orbital path.
Energy Allowed energy states. Allowed quantum states, with energies depending on the system.
Use and limitations Explains key hydrogen spectral lines; limited for more complex atoms. Broader framework for atoms; calculations may require approximations.
The development of quantum theory involved many scientists. Planck, Bohr, and Schrödinger made different contributions at different stages; no single diagram captures all the evidence.

Challenge · Compare models

A classmate says, “A quantum orbital is the same as a Bohr orbit because both refer to electrons.” Explain one similarity and one difference.

Show a possible answer

Both are used to describe electron states in an atom and connect to allowed energies. A Bohr orbit is a specified path in that model; a quantum orbital is associated with a wavefunction and probability distribution rather than a fixed path.

7. Key terms and takeaways

  • Quantum: a discrete amount of energy; for a photon, E = hν.
  • Bohr model: allowed energy states explain key features of the hydrogen spectrum, with limitations for more complex atoms.
  • Transition: absorption can raise the atom’s energy; emission can accompany a decrease, with photon energy equal to the gap.
  • Quantum orbital: a wavefunction-based description associated with a probability distribution, not a fixed orbit.
  • Scientific models: evidence and predictions guide their development and testing.
