Calculating the Amounts of Substances

Real-World Scenario

A laboratory technician needs to prepare a chemical solution using a specific amount of substance. The technician cannot count atoms or molecules directly, so the amount must be calculated using moles, mass, and molar mass. In chemistry, calculating the amount of substance allows us to connect what we measure in the laboratory to the number of particles present in a sample.

Learning Objectives

  • Define amount of substance in terms of moles.
  • Explain the relationship between mass, molar mass, and moles.
  • Calculate moles from mass and molar mass.
  • Calculate mass from moles and molar mass.
  • Solve multi-step problems involving amount of substance.

Amount of Substance

The amount of substance tells how many particles are present in a sample. In chemistry, this amount is measured in moles.

One mole contains Avogadro’s number of particles:

1 mol = 6.022 × 1023 particles
The mole allows chemists to count particles by measuring mass.

This is important because atoms and molecules are too small to count one by one. Instead, chemists use mass and molar mass to determine how many moles are present.

Mass, Moles, and Molar Mass

The relationship among mass, moles, and molar mass is one of the most important tools in chemistry calculations.

Quantity Symbol Common Unit
Mass m grams (g)
Amount of Substance n moles (mol)
Molar Mass M grams per mole (g/mol)
n = m / M
Meaning: Moles are calculated by dividing the given mass by the molar mass.

You can also rearrange the formula:

m = n × M

Use this when you need to calculate the mass of a substance from a given number of moles.

Understanding Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance. It is usually obtained from the periodic table.

Examples

  • Carbon (C): 12.01 g/mol
  • Oxygen (O): 16.00 g/mol
  • Sodium (Na): 22.99 g/mol
  • Water (H₂O): 18.02 g/mol

For elements, the molar mass is taken directly from the periodic table. For compounds, the molar mass is found by adding the molar masses of all atoms in the formula.

Worked Example 1: Finding Moles from Mass

A sample contains 24.0 g of carbon. How many moles of carbon are present?

Step 1: Identify the given values.

Mass = 24.0 g
Molar mass of carbon = 12.01 g/mol

Step 2: Use the formula.

n = m / M

Step 3: Substitute values.

n = 24.0 g / 12.01 g/mol

Step 4: Solve.

n = 2.00 mol
Answer: 24.0 g of carbon is approximately 2.00 mol of carbon.

Worked Example 2: Finding Mass from Moles

How many grams are in 0.500 mol of sodium chloride (NaCl)?

Step 1: Determine molar mass.

Na = 22.99 g/mol
Cl = 35.45 g/mol

Molar mass of NaCl = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol

Step 2: Use the formula.

m = n × M

Step 3: Substitute values.

m = 0.500 mol × 58.44 g/mol

Step 4: Solve.

m = 29.22 g
Answer: 0.500 mol of NaCl has a mass of 29.22 g.

Worked Example 3: Compound Sample

A sample contains 36.04 g of water (H₂O). How many moles of water are present?

Step 1: Calculate molar mass of H₂O.

Hydrogen = 1.01 g/mol
Oxygen = 16.00 g/mol

Molar mass of H₂O = (2 × 1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol

Step 2: Use the formula.

n = m / M

Step 3: Substitute values.

n = 36.04 g / 18.02 g/mol

Step 4: Solve.

n = 2.00 mol
Answer: 36.04 g of water is equal to 2.00 mol of H₂O.

Common Mistakes

  • Using atomic number instead of molar mass.
  • Forgetting to calculate the molar mass of a compound before solving.
  • Using grams when the problem asks for moles.
  • Forgetting that units should cancel properly.

Check Your Understanding

1. How many moles are in 48.0 g of oxygen gas (O₂)?

Step 1: Find molar mass of O₂.

O₂ = 2 × 16.00 = 32.00 g/mol

Step 2: Use n = m / M.

n = 48.0 / 32.00 = 1.50 mol

Answer: 1.50 mol of O₂

2. What is the mass of 3.00 mol of CO₂?

Step 1: Find molar mass of CO₂.

CO₂ = 12.01 + (2 × 16.00) = 44.01 g/mol

Step 2: Use m = n × M.

m = 3.00 × 44.01 = 132.03 g

Answer: 132.03 g of CO₂

3. A student has 10.0 g of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). Estimate the number of moles present.

Step 1: Find molar mass of CaCO₃.

CaCO₃ = 40.08 + 12.01 + (3 × 16.00) = 100.09 g/mol

Step 2: Use n = m / M.

n = 10.0 / 100.09 = 0.0999 mol

Answer: approximately 0.100 mol of CaCO₃

4. Why must molar mass be written in g/mol?

Molar mass tells how many grams are present in one mole of a substance. The unit g/mol allows grams to cancel when calculating moles.

5. Which has more moles: 10 g of carbon or 10 g of sodium? Explain.

Carbon has more moles because it has a smaller molar mass.

Carbon: 10 / 12.01 ≈ 0.833 mol
Sodium: 10 / 22.99 ≈ 0.435 mol

Lesson Summary

  • The mole measures the amount of substance.
  • Molar mass connects grams and moles.
  • To find moles, divide mass by molar mass.
  • To find mass, multiply moles by molar mass.
  • For compounds, calculate molar mass before solving.
  • Unit cancellation helps confirm that the calculation is set up correctly.
