Half-Life, Medical Applications, and Risks

How can a radioactive tracer help doctors see inside the body? Learn how half-life helps describe radioactive decay and why benefits and risks must be considered together.

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain half-life and distinguish random individual decay from predictable sample behavior.
  • Calculate the amount or percentage of parent isotope remaining after a given number of half-lives.
  • Use elapsed time and half-life together and distinguish remaining from decayed amounts.
  • Describe examples of medical imaging and treatment using radioisotopes.
  • Explain how exposure, dose, and protective measures relate to risk.
Connection to Lesson 5: Radioisotopes have unstable nuclei and can emit ionizing radiation. Half-life describes the timing of nuclear decay, while the type and energy of radiation affect its use and hazards.

1. What does half-life mean?

The physical half-life is the time required for half the radioactive nuclei of a given nuclide in a large sample to decay. The amount of the original parent nuclide and its activity each fall by half over one physical half-life, assuming no addition or removal and no other process changes the sample.

Elapsed half-lives Fraction of parent remaining Percent remaining Example: starting with 80 g
0 1 100% 80 g
1 1/2 50% 40 g
2 1/4 25% 20 g
3 1/8 12.5% 10 g
4 1/16 6.25% 5 g

The sample is halved repeatedly; it does not lose the same original amount at every step. Each nucleus decays randomly, but the behavior of a large population is predictable.

After one half-life, half the parent nuclei remain; the other half have changed into daughter products. The material does not simply vanish, and the model does not imply that the whole sample is nonradioactive after one or two half-lives. Daughter products may themselves be radioactive.

2. Solve half-life problems

number of half-lives, n = elapsed time ÷ half-life
parent amount remaining = initial amount × (1/2)n

Use consistent time units. For elapsed times that are whole numbers of half-lives, repeated halving is often the easiest method.

Example 1 · Amount remaining

A sample begins with 120 g of a parent radioisotope. Its half-life is 4 hours. How much parent isotope remains after 12 hours?

  1. n = 12 h ÷ 4 h = 3 half-lives.
  2. 120 g → 60 g → 30 g → 15 g.
  3. Check: 120 × (1/2)³ = 15 g. This represents 12.5% remaining.

Example 2 · Percentage decayed

A sample has a half-life of 10 days. After 20 days, what percentage of the initial parent nuclei has decayed?

20 ÷ 10 = 2 half-lives. The remaining fraction is (1/2)² = 1/4 = 25%. The fraction that has decayed is 100% − 25% = 75%.

Example 3 · Find elapsed time

A sample decreases from 200 mg to 25 mg of parent isotope. If its half-life is 3 days, how much time has elapsed?

200 → 100 → 50 → 25 mg: three halvings. Time = 3 × 3 days = 9 days.

Try it

A 64 mg parent sample has a half-life of 6 hours. How much remains after 18 hours?

Show answer

18 ÷ 6 = 3 half-lives. 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 mg remains.

3. Why radioisotopes are useful in medicine

In nuclear medicine, a radiopharmaceutical combines a radioactive nuclide with a chemical form chosen for its biological behavior. Tracers can help image bodily processes; selected radioactive substances can also deliver treatment. The medical team chooses and controls the procedure.

Example Use and principle Half-life connection
Technetium-99m Used in many diagnostic imaging studies; gamma photons emitted by the tracer can be detected by specialized cameras. Physical half-life about 6 hours: long enough for preparation and imaging but short enough for activity to decrease relatively quickly.
Iodine-131 Radioiodine can be used to treat an overactive thyroid or certain thyroid cancers. Thyroid tissue takes up iodine; emitted beta radiation contributes to the treatment. Physical half-life about 8 days; duration, uptake, dose, and follow-up all matter.
Cobalt-60 A sealed source emits gamma radiation that can be directed at tumors in external-beam radiotherapy. Its longer half-life supports use as a source over time; the patient does not take the sealed source into the body.
A short physical half-life alone does not guarantee a low dose or suitability. Radiation type, energy, administered activity, chemical form, organ uptake, and how quickly the body removes the material matter. Biological elimination and physical decay together affect how long activity remains in the body.

Think like a scientist

Why might a gamma-emitting tracer be useful for imaging?

Show a possible answer

Gamma photons can travel out of the body and be detected by an external camera, allowing the distribution of a suitable tracer to be imaged. The procedure still involves exposure and must be justified and controlled.

4. Radiation risks and protection

Ionizing radiation can damage cells and DNA. Health effects depend on absorbed dose, dose rate, radiation type and energy, affected tissue, and whether exposure is internal or external. Higher doses can cause tissue injury; radiation exposure can also increase the chance of later cancer. The nature and magnitude of risk depend on the circumstances.

Protection principle Meaning Example
Time Limit unnecessary time near a source. A trained worker prepares before entering an area.
Distance Increase distance from an external source when practical. Use remote handling equipment as directed.
Shielding Use suitable material and thickness for the radiation. Facilities select appropriate barriers based on source and radiation energy.
Prevent contamination Keep radioactive material from entering or spreading onto the body and surroundings. Follow institutional procedures for containment and monitoring.

Exposure versus contamination: Exposure means radiation reaches the body. Contamination means radioactive material is on or inside a person or object. An external exposure does not necessarily make a person radioactive.

These are general educational principles. Medical use and radioactive sources require trained professionals, applicable local rules, and specific procedures; a patient should follow their care team’s instructions.

Challenge · Apply your understanding

A 96 mg sample has a half-life of 5 days. How much parent isotope remains after 15 days, and what percentage has decayed?

Show answer

15 ÷ 5 = 3 half-lives; 96 → 48 → 24 → 12 mg remains. The remaining fraction is 1/8 = 12.5%, so 87.5% has decayed.

6. Key takeaways

  • A physical half-life is the time for half the parent nuclei in a large sample to decay.
  • Remaining fraction = (1/2)n, where n = elapsed time ÷ half-life.
  • Radioisotopes can support imaging or therapy; choice depends on several physical and biological factors.
  • Radiation risk depends on exposure and dose; trained professionals use time, distance, shielding, and contamination control.
