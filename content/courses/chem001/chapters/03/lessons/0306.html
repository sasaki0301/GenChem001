Chapter Summary: The Mole Concept and Chemical Quantities

Chapter Big Idea

Chemists cannot directly count atoms, molecules, or ions because these particles are extremely small and exist in enormous numbers. The mole concept provides a bridge between the microscopic world of particles and the macroscopic world of measurable quantities such as mass. By understanding moles, molar mass, particle counting, empirical formulas, molecular formulas, and percent composition, chemists can determine the composition and quantity of substances involved in chemical processes.

1. The Mole Concept and Avogadro's Number

The mole is the SI unit used to measure the amount of substance. It functions as a counting unit in chemistry, similar to how a dozen represents 12 objects.

1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ representative particles

This number is known as Avogadro's Number.

Substance Type Representative Particle
Elements Atoms
Molecular Compounds Molecules
Ionic Compounds Formula Units

Examples

  • 1 mol Fe = 6.022 × 10²³ iron atoms
  • 1 mol H₂O = 6.022 × 10²³ water molecules
  • 1 mol NaCl = 6.022 × 10²³ formula units

2. Converting Between Moles and Particles

Avogadro's number allows us to convert between moles and particles.

Particles = Moles × 6.022 × 10²³

Moles = Particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³

Example

How many molecules are in 2.00 mol of CO₂?

2.00 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ molecules

3. Amount of Substance and Molar Mass

Molar mass is the mass of one mole of a substance and provides the connection between mass and amount of substance.

Unit of Molar Mass: g/mol

Relationship among mass, moles, and molar mass:

n = m ÷ M

m = n × M
Symbol Meaning
n Moles
m Mass
M Molar Mass

Example

24.0 g C ÷ 12.01 g/mol = 2.00 mol C

4. Counting Atoms and Molecules

Chemical formulas indicate how many atoms of each element are present in a molecule or formula unit.

Formula Total Atoms
H₂O 3
CO₂ 3
NH₃ 4
C₆H₁₂O₆ 24
Subscripts indicate the number of atoms of each element present in one molecule.

When counting atoms from moles, first determine the number of molecules and then multiply by the appropriate subscript.

Example

How many hydrogen atoms are present in 1.00 mol of H₂O?

6.022 × 10²³ molecules × 2 = 1.204 × 10²⁴ hydrogen atoms

5. Empirical and Molecular Formulas

The molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.
Molecular Formula = (Empirical Formula)n

n = Molecular Mass ÷ Empirical Formula Mass

6. Determining Empirical Formulas from Percent Composition

Chemists often determine formulas from experimental composition data.

General Procedure:
  1. Assume a 100 g sample.
  2. Convert grams to moles.
  3. Divide all mole values by the smallest value.
  4. Convert ratios to whole numbers.
  5. Write the empirical formula.

Example

40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O

After conversion and simplification:

CH₂O

7. Molar Mass of Compounds

The molar mass of a compound is obtained by adding the molar masses of all atoms in the formula.

Example: Water

(2 × 1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol

Example: Carbon Dioxide

12.01 + (2 × 16.00) = 44.01 g/mol

8. Percent Composition

Percent composition describes the percentage by mass contributed by each element in a compound.

Percent Composition = (Element Mass ÷ Compound Molar Mass) × 100

Example: Water

% H = (2.02 ÷ 18.02) × 100 = 11.21%

% O = (16.00 ÷ 18.02) × 100 = 88.79%
The percentages of all elements in a compound should total approximately 100%.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Confusing atoms, molecules, and formula units.
  • Using multiplication instead of division when converting particles to moles.
  • Ignoring subscripts when counting atoms.
  • Using atomic number instead of atomic mass.
  • Forgetting to calculate molar mass before determining percent composition.
  • Confusing empirical formulas with molecular formulas.
  • Rounding mole ratios too early when deriving formulas.

5. Why is percent composition useful?

Percent composition helps determine the composition, purity, and identity of chemical substances.

Chapter Summary

  • The mole is the SI unit used to measure the amount of substance.
  • One mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ representative particles.
  • Molar mass connects grams and moles.
  • Particle counting requires Avogadro's number and proper interpretation of chemical formulas.
  • Empirical formulas show the simplest atom ratios, while molecular formulas show actual atom counts.
  • Percent composition reveals how much each element contributes to a compound's mass.
  • Together, these concepts allow chemists to determine the composition and quantity of substances accurately.
