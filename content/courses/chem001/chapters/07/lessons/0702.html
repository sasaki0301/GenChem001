Applying the Trends in Atomic Properties

Real-World Scenario

Why does fluorine attract electrons more strongly than sodium? Why is potassium more reactive than lithium? Why do noble gases rarely form compounds? The answers can be predicted using periodic trends. The arrangement of elements in the periodic table is not random. As we move across periods and down groups, several atomic properties change in predictable ways. Understanding these trends allows chemists and engineers to predict reactivity, bonding behavior, and material properties without performing experiments.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the major periodic trends.
  • Describe changes in atomic radius.
  • Differentiate atomic and ionic radius.
  • Explain ionization energy.
  • Interpret electron affinity values.
  • Describe electronegativity trends.
  • Explain metallic character.
  • Predict reactivity using periodic trends.

Why Do Periodic Trends Exist?

Periodic trends arise because of changes in nuclear charge and electron arrangement as atomic number increases.

Two major factors control periodic trends:
  • Effective Nuclear Charge – the attraction between the nucleus and outer electrons.
  • Electron Shielding – inner electrons reduce the attraction felt by outer electrons.

The competition between nuclear attraction and shielding produces predictable patterns across the periodic table.

Atomic Radius

Atomic radius is the size of an atom, typically measured as half the distance between two bonded nuclei.

Atomic radius measures how large an atom is.

Trend in Atomic Radius

Direction Trend
Across a Period → Decreases
Down a Group ↓ Increases
Across a Period:
  • More protons are added.
  • Nuclear attraction increases.
  • Electrons are pulled closer.
  • Atomic size decreases.
Down a Group:
  • Additional energy levels are added.
  • Electron shielding increases.
  • Outer electrons are farther from the nucleus.
  • Atomic size increases.

Example: Atomic Radius

Across Period 3

Na → Mg → Al → Si → P → S → Cl

Atomic radius decreases from left to right.

Down Group 1

Li → Na → K → Rb

Atomic radius increases as additional energy levels are added.

Ionic Radius

Ionic radius refers to the size of an ion after electrons have been gained or lost.

Cations (positive ions) become smaller.

Anions (negative ions) become larger.

Example: Ionic Radius

Sodium

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Na⁺ is smaller than Na because an outer electron shell is lost.

Chlorine

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Cl⁻ is larger than Cl because the added electron increases electron-electron repulsion.

Ionization Energy

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom.

High ionization energy means electrons are held strongly by the nucleus.

Trend in Ionization Energy

Direction Trend
Across a Period → Increases
Down a Group ↓ Decreases

Atoms with smaller radii generally have higher ionization energies because their electrons are closer to the nucleus.

Example: Ionization Energy

Group 1 Comparison

Li > Na > K

Lithium requires more energy to remove an electron because its valence electron is closer to the nucleus.

Electron Affinity

Electron affinity measures the tendency of an atom to gain an electron.

The more negative the electron affinity, the stronger the attraction for additional electrons.

Trend in Electron Affinity

Direction General Trend
Across a Period → More Negative
Down a Group ↓ Less Negative

Halogens possess some of the highest electron affinities because they need only one electron to complete their valence shell.

Example: Electron Affinity

Fluorine and chlorine readily gain electrons because they are only one electron short of a complete octet.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Electronegativity

Electronegativity measures an atom's ability to attract shared electrons in a chemical bond.

Electronegativity is one of the most important concepts for predicting bond polarity.

Trend in Electronegativity

Direction Trend
Across a Period → Increases
Down a Group ↓ Decreases

Highest Electronegativity

Fluorine (F)

Fluorine attracts bonding electrons more strongly than any other element.

Metallic Character

Metallic character refers to how readily an atom loses electrons and exhibits metallic properties.

Trend in Metallic Character

Direction Trend
Across a Period → Decreases
Down a Group ↓ Increases
Most Metallic Region:

Bottom Left of the Periodic Table

(Cs, Fr)
Least Metallic Region:

Upper Right of the Periodic Table

(F, O, Cl)

Summary of Major Trends

Property Across a Period → Down a Group ↓
Atomic Radius Decreases Increases
Ionization Energy Increases Decreases
Electron Affinity More Negative Less Negative
Electronegativity Increases Decreases
Metallic Character Decreases Increases

Using Trends to Predict Reactivity

Alkali Metals

Li < Na < K < Rb

Reactivity increases down the group because valence electrons are easier to remove.

Halogens

F > Cl > Br > I

Reactivity decreases down the group because attraction for incoming electrons decreases.

Check Your Understanding

1. Which atom is larger: Na or Cl?

Na is larger because atomic radius decreases across a period.

2. Which element has the highest electronegativity?

Fluorine (F).

3. Does ionization energy generally increase or decrease across a period?

It generally increases.

4. Which ion is larger: Cl or Cl⁻?

Cl⁻ is larger.

5. Which alkali metal is more reactive: Li or K?

K is more reactive.

6. Which halogen is more reactive: F or I?

F is more reactive.

7. Which element has a larger atomic radius: Mg or Ca?

Ca because atomic radius increases down a group.

8. Which element has higher ionization energy: Na or Cl?

Cl because ionization energy increases across a period.

9. Why are noble gases generally unreactive?

Because they already possess complete valence shells.

10. Which corner of the periodic table contains the most metallic elements?

The lower-left corner.

11. Rank these elements from smallest to largest atomic radius: Na, Mg, Cl.

Cl < Mg < Na

12. Rank these elements from highest to lowest electronegativity: F, O, N.

F > O > N

Lesson Summary

  • Periodic trends arise from changes in nuclear charge and electron shielding.
  • Atomic radius decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • Cations are smaller than their parent atoms, while anions are larger.
  • Ionization energy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Electron affinity becomes more favorable across a period.
  • Electronegativity increases across a period and decreases down a group.
  • Metallic character decreases across a period and increases down a group.
  • Periodic trends help predict reactivity, bonding, and chemical behavior.
