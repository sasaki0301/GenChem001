Using the Periodic Table to Identify Atomic Properties

Real-World Scenario

Every material around us—smartphones, medicines, batteries, construction materials, food nutrients, and even the air we breathe—is made of elements found in the periodic table. Chemists and engineers use the periodic table as a powerful tool to predict an element's properties, behavior, bonding tendencies, and reactivity. Understanding how to read the periodic table allows us to quickly determine important information about any element.

Learning Objectives

  • Describe the organization of the periodic table.
  • Identify periods, groups, and blocks.
  • Differentiate metals, nonmetals, and metalloids.
  • Recognize major element families.
  • Determine atomic number, protons, neutrons, and electrons.
  • Determine valence electrons.
  • Predict common ionic charges.
  • Use the periodic table to infer atomic properties.

What is the Periodic Table?

The periodic table is an organized arrangement of elements based on increasing atomic number and recurring chemical properties.

The periodic table organizes elements so that those with similar chemical behavior appear in the same vertical column.

Modern periodic law states that the physical and chemical properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

Periods and Groups

The periodic table is arranged into horizontal rows and vertical columns.

Structure Description
Period Horizontal row
Group (Family) Vertical column
Elements in the same group generally have:
  • Similar valence electrons
  • Similar chemical properties
  • Similar bonding behavior

Periods and Energy Levels

The period number corresponds to the highest occupied principal energy level of an atom.

Examples

  • Na is in Period 3 → highest occupied shell = n = 3
  • K is in Period 4 → highest occupied shell = n = 4
  • Ca is in Period 4 → highest occupied shell = n = 4

Major Regions of the Periodic Table

Category General Characteristics

Metals

Metals occupy the left and center portions of the periodic table.

Common Properties:
  • Good electrical conductors
  • Good heat conductors
  • Malleable
  • Ductile
  • Usually form positive ions
Examples:
  • Na (Sodium)
  • Mg (Magnesium)
  • Al (Aluminum)
  • Fe (Iron)
  • Cu (Copper)

Nonmetals

Nonmetals occupy the upper-right region of the periodic table.

Common Properties:
  • Poor conductors
  • Often brittle
  • Tend to gain electrons
  • Usually form negative ions
Examples:
  • O (Oxygen)
  • N (Nitrogen)
  • Cl (Chlorine)
  • S (Sulfur)

Metalloids

Metalloids exhibit properties intermediate between metals and nonmetals.

Common Metalloids:
  • Boron (B)
  • Silicon (Si)
  • Germanium (Ge)
  • Arsenic (As)
  • Antimony (Sb)
Many metalloids are important semiconductors used in electronics.

Atomic Number, Protons, Neutrons, and Electrons

The periodic table allows us to determine the fundamental particles of atoms.

Atomic Number = Protons
Electrons = Protons (for neutral atoms)
Neutrons = Mass Number − Atomic Number

Example 1: Oxygen

Atomic Number = 8

Mass Number ≈ 16

  • Protons = 8
  • Electrons = 8
  • Neutrons = 16 − 8 = 8

Example 2: Sodium

Atomic Number = 11

Mass Number ≈ 23

  • Protons = 11
  • Electrons = 11
  • Neutrons = 12

Valence Electrons

Valence electrons are the electrons in the outermost occupied energy level.

Valence electrons largely determine an element's chemical properties and bonding behavior.

Predicting Common Ionic Charges

Atoms tend to gain or lose electrons to achieve stable noble-gas configurations.

Group Common Ion
1 +1
2 +2
13 +3
15 -3
16 -2
17 -1

Example: Predicting Ions

Sodium belongs to Group 1.

It loses one electron.

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Chlorine belongs to Group 17.

It gains one electron.

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Magnesium belongs to Group 2.

Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻

Using the Periodic Table as a Prediction Tool

From the periodic table, we can predict:
  • Number of valence electrons
  • Likely ion charge
  • Metallic or nonmetallic character
  • Chemical reactivity
  • Electron configuration patterns
  • Bonding behavior

Check Your Understanding

1. How many valence electrons does sulfur have?

Sulfur belongs to Group 16, so it has 6 valence electrons.

2. What ion is commonly formed by calcium?

Ca²⁺ because calcium belongs to Group 2 and loses two electrons.

3. How many protons does phosphorus contain?

15 protons because its atomic number is 15.

4. Classify silicon as a metal, nonmetal, or metalloid.

Silicon is a metalloid.

5. Which family does chlorine belong to?

The halogen family (Group 17).

6. Determine the number of neutrons in sodium-23.

23 − 11 = 12 neutrons.

7. Which group contains the most chemically stable elements?

Group 18 (Noble Gases).

8. What ion will oxygen typically form?

O²⁻

9. An element belongs to Group 1. How many valence electrons does it have?

1 valence electron.

10. Why are noble gases generally unreactive?

Because they already possess filled valence shells.

Lesson Summary

  • The periodic table organizes elements according to atomic number and recurring properties.
  • Periods are horizontal rows, while groups are vertical columns.
  • Elements are classified as metals, nonmetals, or metalloids.
  • Elements in the same group share similar valence electrons and chemical behavior.
  • The periodic table can be used to determine protons, electrons, neutrons, and valence electrons.
  • Valence electrons largely determine bonding and reactivity.
  • Common ionic charges can often be predicted directly from group number.
  • The periodic table is one of chemistry's most powerful predictive tools.
