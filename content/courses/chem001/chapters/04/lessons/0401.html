Balancing and Writing Chemical Reactions

Real-World Scenario

When hydrogen fuel burns in oxygen, water is produced. Engineers designing fuel cells must know exactly how much hydrogen and oxygen are required and how much water will form. To do this accurately, chemists use chemical equations. Chemical equations are the language of chemistry. They describe what substances react, what products form, and the quantitative relationships between them.

Learning Objectives

  • Identify reactants and products in a chemical reaction.
  • Interpret chemical equations.
  • Write word equations and chemical equations.
  • Apply the Law of Conservation of Mass.
  • Balance chemical equations using coefficients.
  • Distinguish between coefficients and subscripts.

What is a Chemical Reaction?

A chemical reaction is a process in which one or more substances are transformed into new substances with different compositions and properties.

During a chemical reaction:
  • Old chemical bonds are broken.
  • Atoms are rearranged.
  • New chemical bonds are formed.
  • No atoms are created or destroyed.

Although the substances change, the total number of atoms remains the same.

Reactants and Products

Every chemical reaction contains two important parts:

Term Description
Reactants Substances present before the reaction begins
Products Substances formed after the reaction occurs

Example

Hydrogen reacts with oxygen to produce water.

Hydrogen + Oxygen → Water
  • Reactants: Hydrogen and Oxygen
  • Product: Water

Word Equations

A word equation describes a chemical reaction using the names of substances.

Examples

Hydrogen + Oxygen → Water

Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium Oxide

Methane + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water

Word equations help us understand what substances are involved, but they do not show the exact chemical formulas.

Chemical Equations

A chemical equation uses chemical symbols and formulas instead of words.

Examples

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Mg + O₂ → MgO

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Chemical equations provide more information because they show the exact formulas of the substances involved.

Understanding the Parts of a Chemical Equation

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Symbol Meaning
+ Reacts with
→ Produces or yields
2H₂ 2 molecules of hydrogen gas
O₂ 1 molecule of oxygen gas
2H₂O 2 molecules of water

The Law of Conservation of Mass

The Law of Conservation of Mass states that matter cannot be created or destroyed during a chemical reaction.

The number of atoms of each element must be the same before and after a chemical reaction.

This is why chemical equations must be balanced.

Unbalanced Equation

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
Element Reactants Products
H 2 2
O 2 1

The oxygen atoms are not equal, so the equation is not balanced.

Balancing Chemical Equations

Balancing an equation means adjusting coefficients so that the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides.

Only coefficients may be changed when balancing equations.

Subscripts must NEVER be changed.

Coefficients vs Subscripts

Coefficient Subscript
Placed in front of a formula Part of the chemical formula
Can be changed when balancing Cannot be changed
Changes the number of molecules Changes the identity of the substance

Example

2H₂O

Coefficient = 2
Subscript = 2

This represents:

  • 4 hydrogen atoms
  • 2 oxygen atoms

Worked Example 1

Balance:

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Step 1: Count atoms.

Element Reactants Products
H 2 2
O 2 1

Step 2: Balance oxygen.

H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Now:

  • H = 2 on left, 4 on right
  • O = 2 on left, 2 on right

Step 3: Balance hydrogen.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Final answer:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Worked Example 2

Balance:

N₂ + H₂ → NH₃

Step 1: Balance nitrogen.

N₂ + H₂ → 2NH₃

Step 2: Balance hydrogen.

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Final balanced equation:

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

General Strategy for Balancing Equations

  1. Write the correct chemical formulas.
  2. Count atoms on both sides.
  3. Balance one element at a time.
  4. Use coefficients only.
  5. Check all atom counts.
  6. Reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio if necessary.

Common Mistakes

  • Changing subscripts instead of coefficients.
  • Forgetting to count atoms multiplied by coefficients.
  • Balancing one element while unbalancing another.
  • Ignoring diatomic elements such as H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, Br₂, and I₂.
  • Not checking the final atom counts.

Check Your Understanding

1. Identify the reactants and products in:
C + O₂ → CO₂

Reactants:
  • Carbon (C)
  • Oxygen (O₂)
Product:
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

2. Why can coefficients be changed but not subscripts?

Changing coefficients changes the number of molecules. Changing subscripts changes the identity of the substance itself.

3. Balance:
Mg + O₂ → MgO

2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

4. Balance:
Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃

4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃

5. Why must chemical equations be balanced?

Because matter is conserved during chemical reactions. The number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides of the equation.

6. Balance the following equation:

Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃

Step 1: Balance aluminum.

4Al + O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Step 2: Balance oxygen.

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Balanced Equation:

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

7. Balance the following equation:

C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Step 1: Balance carbon.

C₂H₆ + O₂ → 2CO₂ + H₂O

Step 2: Balance hydrogen.

C₂H₆ + O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Step 3: Balance oxygen.

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

Balanced Equation:

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

8. Balance the following equation containing a polyatomic ion:

Na₃PO₄ + MgCl₂ → Mg₃(PO₄)₂ + NaCl

Notice that the phosphate ion (PO₄) remains unchanged on both sides, so treat it as a single unit.

2Na₃PO₄ + 3MgCl₂ → Mg₃(PO₄)₂ + 6NaCl

Balanced Equation:

2Na₃PO₄ + 3MgCl₂ → Mg₃(PO₄)₂ + 6NaCl

9. Balance the following equation containing a polyatomic ion:

Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + KOH → Fe(OH)₃ + K₂SO₄

Treat sulfate (SO₄) as a single unit because it appears unchanged on both sides.

Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 6KOH → 2Fe(OH)₃ + 3K₂SO₄

Balanced Equation:

Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 6KOH → 2Fe(OH)₃ + 3K₂SO₄

10. Balance the following equation:

Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + SiO₂ + C → P₄ + CaSiO₃ + CO

This is an industrial phosphorus-production reaction and requires balancing several elements systematically.

2Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 6SiO₂ + 10C → P₄ + 6CaSiO₃ + 10CO

Balanced Equation:

2Ca₃(PO₄)₂ + 6SiO₂ + 10C → P₄ + 6CaSiO₃ + 10CO

Lesson Summary

  • Chemical reactions transform reactants into products.
  • Word equations describe reactions using names, while chemical equations use formulas.
  • The Law of Conservation of Mass requires the same number of atoms before and after a reaction.
  • Chemical equations are balanced by changing coefficients, never subscripts.
  • Balanced equations show the quantitative relationships among reactants and products.
  • Balancing equations is the foundation of stoichiometric calculations.
