Chemical Formulas and Molar Mass

A chemical formula tells us which elements are present and in what ratios. How can we use that information to determine the mass of one mole of a substance?

ObjectivesRead formulasParenthesesThe moleMolar massWorked examplesPractice

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Interpret element symbols, subscripts, coefficients, and parentheses in chemical formulas.
  • Count the atoms of each element in a molecule or formula unit.
  • Explain the relationship between the mole, formula mass, and molar mass.
  • Calculate molar mass and each element’s mass contribution from a chemical formula.
  • Use molar mass to convert between moles and grams.
Connection: Chapter 3 explored atomic mass and isotopes. Here we add the contributions of the atoms in a formula. These mass contributions prepare us for percent composition in Lesson 2.

1. Read a chemical formula

A chemical formula uses element symbols and subscripts to describe the relative numbers of atoms in a substance. Element symbols are case-sensitive: Co is cobalt, while CO contains carbon and oxygen.

The coefficient changes the number of formula entities, not the composition of one entity.

Formula One molecule or formula unit contains Interpretation
CO2 1 carbon atom and 2 oxygen atoms The subscript applies to the element just before it.
NaCl 1 Na and 1 Cl per formula unit No subscript means 1. An ionic formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of ions; it does not represent a discrete NaCl molecule.
2H2O Two water molecules: 4 H and 2 O atoms in total The coefficient multiplies the entire formula. One H2O molecule still contains 2 H and 1 O.
Do not change a formula’s subscripts to change the number of molecules. For example, 2H2O and H2O2 describe different things.

2. Count atoms when formulas contain parentheses

A subscript outside parentheses multiplies every atom inside the parentheses. An element outside the parentheses is counted separately.

Worked example · Calcium hydroxide

Ca(OH)2 contains:

  • Ca: 1 atom
  • O: 1 × 2 = 2 atoms
  • H: 1 × 2 = 2 atoms

One formula unit has 5 atoms in total. The 2 outside the parentheses applies to both O and H, not to Ca.

Worked example · Aluminum sulfate

In Al2(SO4)3: Al = 2, S = 1 × 3 = 3, and O = 4 × 3 = 12 atoms per formula unit. There are 17 atoms altogether.

Try it first

How many nitrogen and hydrogen atoms are in one formula unit of (NH4)2SO4?

Show answer

N = 1 × 2 = 2; H = 4 × 2 = 8. The formula also contains S = 1 and O = 4.

3. Connect formula mass to molar mass

A mole is an amount of substance containing exactly 6.02214076 × 1023 specified elementary entities. Depending on the substance, the entities might be atoms, molecules, or formula units.

Quantity Meaning Example
Formula mass Sum of atomic masses for the atoms in one specified formula, expressed in unified atomic mass units (u). One H2O molecule has a molecular mass of approximately 18.02 u.
Molar mass Mass per mole of specified entities, expressed in g/mol. One mole of H2O molecules has a mass of approximately 18.02 g.

The numerical values correspond when the same atomic-mass data are used, but the quantities and units differ. For an ionic compound such as NaCl, use formula unit and formula mass; molar mass still has units of g/mol.

Molar mass is mass per mole. It is not the total mass of every sample: 2.00 mol of water has twice the mass of 1.00 mol of water.

4. Find molar mass step by step

Use the rounded atomic molar masses below throughout this lesson. A different periodic table or rounding convention may give a slightly different result.

Element H C N O Na Mg S Cl Ca Al
g/mol 1.01 12.01 14.01 16.00 22.99 24.31 32.06 35.45 40.08 26.98
  1. Read the formula and count each type of atom in one molecule or formula unit.
  2. Find each element’s atomic molar mass in g/mol.
  3. Multiply each atomic molar mass by the number of atoms of that element in the formula. This gives that element’s mass contribution per mole of compound.
  4. Add all contributions and report the molar mass in g/mol.

Molar mass = Σ (number of atoms × atomic molar mass)

For 1 mol of CO2, there are 1 mol C atoms and 2 mol O atoms. Therefore the oxygen contribution is 2 × 16.00 = 32.00 g per mol of CO2.

5. Worked examples

Example 1 · Water, H2O

Element Atoms per molecule Calculation Contribution per mole of H2O
H 2 2 × 1.01 2.02 g
O 1 1 × 16.00 16.00 g
Total molar mass 18.02 g/mol

Oxygen contributes more mass even though there are more H atoms. The number of atoms and the atomic molar mass both matter.

Example 2 · Calcium carbonate, CaCO3

Ca: 1 × 40.08 = 40.08 g/mol
C: 1 × 12.01 = 12.01 g/mol
O: 3 × 16.00 = 48.00 g/mol

Total = 40.08 + 12.01 + 48.00 = 100.09 g/mol. These element contributions are the starting point for percent composition in Lesson 2.

Example 3 · Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2

Count first: Ca = 1, O = 2, H = 2.

M = (1 × 40.08) + (2 × 16.00) + (2 × 1.01)
= 40.08 + 32.00 + 2.02 = 74.10 g/mol.

Example 4 · Convert moles and mass

What is the mass of 2.00 mol of H2O?

Mass = amount × molar mass = 2.00 mol × 18.02 g/mol = 36.04 g ≈ 36.0 g to three significant figures.

For the reverse conversion, amount in mol = mass in g ÷ molar mass in g/mol. For example, 18.02 g of water corresponds to 1.000 mol using this molar mass.

Coefficient check: The molar mass of H2O is 18.02 g/mol whether you write 1H2O or 2H2O. The coefficient indicates a quantity or reaction ratio; it does not double the molar mass of the substance.

Challenge · Analyze a formula

Calculate the molar mass of Al2(SO4)3 using Al = 26.98, S = 32.06 and O = 16.00 g/mol. Identify the element with the greatest mass contribution.

Show one solution

Count Al = 2, S = 3 and O = 12. Contributions: Al = 2(26.98) = 53.96 g/mol; S = 3(32.06) = 96.18 g/mol; O = 12(16.00) = 192.00 g/mol. Total = 342.14 g/mol. Oxygen contributes the most mass per mole of this compound.

7. Key takeaways

  • Subscripts show atom counts; a subscript outside parentheses multiplies all atoms within.
  • A coefficient applies to the entire formula and does not change the substance’s molar mass.
  • A molecular formula describes molecules; an ionic formula gives the simplest ratio in a formula unit.
  • Molar mass is found by adding atomic molar-mass contributions and is reported in g/mol.
  • Mass in grams = amount in moles × molar mass. Divide mass by molar mass to find moles.
  • Each element’s mass contribution will be used to calculate percent composition in Lesson 2.
