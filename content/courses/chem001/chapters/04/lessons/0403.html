Calculating Amounts of Reactants and Products

Real-World Scenario

A chemical engineer needs to determine how much ammonia can be produced from a given amount of nitrogen gas. A pharmaceutical manufacturer needs to know exactly how much reactant is required to produce a specific quantity of medicine. These questions are answered using stoichiometry, the branch of chemistry that deals with quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the meaning of stoichiometry.
  • Interpret mole ratios from balanced chemical equations.
  • Perform mole-to-mole calculations.
  • Perform mass-to-mass calculations.
  • Perform mass-to-mole and mole-to-mass calculations.
  • Apply stoichiometric reasoning to chemical reactions.

What is Stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry is the calculation of the quantities of reactants consumed and products formed during chemical reactions.

Stoichiometry uses the coefficients in a balanced chemical equation to establish quantitative relationships between substances.

A balanced chemical equation acts like a recipe. Just as a recipe specifies how much of each ingredient is needed, a balanced equation specifies how much of each substance participates in a reaction.

The Importance of Balanced Equations

Every stoichiometric calculation begins with a balanced chemical equation.

Example

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

This equation tells us that:

  • 1 mole of N₂ reacts with 3 moles of H₂
  • 1 mole of N₂ produces 2 moles of NH₃
  • 3 moles of H₂ produce 2 moles of NH₃
The coefficients in a balanced equation represent mole ratios.

Mole Ratios

A mole ratio is a conversion factor derived from the coefficients of a balanced chemical equation.

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Possible mole ratios:

  • 1 mol N₂ : 3 mol H₂
  • 1 mol N₂ : 2 mol NH₃
  • 3 mol H₂ : 2 mol NH₃
  • 2 mol NH₃ : 1 mol N₂

These mole ratios are the foundation of all stoichiometric calculations.

General Stoichiometry Roadmap

Most stoichiometry problems follow this sequence:

1. Write and balance the equation.
2. Convert the given quantity to moles.
3. Apply the appropriate mole ratio.
4. Convert to the desired unit.

Mole-to-Mole Calculations

The simplest stoichiometric calculation involves converting between moles of different substances.

Worked Example 1

How many moles of NH₃ can be produced from 5.00 mol of N₂?

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Step 1: Identify the mole ratio.

2 mol NH₃ ────────── 1 mol N₂

Step 2: Apply the ratio.

5.00 mol N₂ × 2 mol NH₃ ────────── 1 mol N₂
= 10.0 mol NH₃

Answer: 10.0 mol NH₃

Mass-to-Mass Calculations

Most laboratory measurements involve mass rather than moles. Therefore, mass-to-mass stoichiometry is one of the most useful applications of chemistry.

Mass-to-Mass Problems: Mass → Moles → Mole Ratio → Moles → Mass

Worked Example 2

How many grams of water are produced when 4.00 g of hydrogen reacts completely with oxygen?

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Step 1: Convert grams H₂ to moles.

4.00 g ÷ 2.02 g/mol = 1.98 mol H₂

Step 2: Apply mole ratio.

1.98 mol H₂ × 2 mol H₂O ────────── 2 mol H₂ = 1.98 mol H₂O

Step 3: Convert moles H₂O to grams.

1.98 × 18.02 = 35.7 g H₂O

Answer: 35.7 g H₂O

Mole-to-Mass Calculations

Sometimes the given quantity is already expressed in moles, and the desired answer is mass.

Worked Example 3

How many grams of CO₂ are produced from 3.00 mol of CH₄?

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Step 1: Apply the mole ratio.

3.00 mol CH₄ × 1 mol CO₂ ────────── 1 mol CH₄ = 3.00 mol CO₂

Step 2: Convert to grams.

3.00 × 44.01 = 132.03 g CO₂

Answer: 132.03 g CO₂

Mass-to-Mole Calculations

In some problems, the given quantity is mass, while the answer is requested in moles.

Worked Example 4

How many moles of oxygen are needed to react completely with 16.0 g of methane?

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Step 1: Convert methane to moles.

16.0 ÷ 16.05 = 0.997 mol CH₄

Step 2: Apply mole ratio.

0.997 × 2 mol O₂ ────────── 1 mol CH₄ = 1.99 mol O₂

Answer: 1.99 mol O₂

Visual Stoichiometry Map

Mass ↓ (Molar Mass) Moles ↓ (Mole Ratio) Moles ↓ (Molar Mass) Mass

Almost every stoichiometry problem follows this pathway.

Common Mistakes

  • Using an unbalanced chemical equation.
  • Using atomic masses instead of molar masses.
  • Applying the wrong mole ratio.
  • Skipping the conversion to moles.
  • Confusing coefficients with subscripts.
  • Using the inverse mole ratio.

Check Your Understanding

1. Using the equation N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, how many moles of NH₃ are produced from 4.00 mol N₂?

4.00 × 2 = 8.00 mol NH₃

2. Using 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how many moles of H₂O are produced from 6.00 mol H₂?

6.00 mol H₂O
The mole ratio is 2:2 or 1:1.

3. How many grams of CO₂ are produced from 1.00 mol CO₂?

1.00 × 44.01 = 44.01 g CO₂

4. Why must every stoichiometric calculation begin with a balanced equation?

Because the coefficients provide the mole ratios needed for calculations.

5. Which conversion factor connects two substances in a chemical equation?

The mole ratio derived from the balanced equation.

Lesson Summary

  • Stoichiometry studies quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.
  • Balanced equations provide mole ratios between substances.
  • Mole ratios are used to convert between reactants and products.
  • Most stoichiometric calculations require converting quantities to moles first.
  • Mass-to-mass problems combine molar mass calculations and mole ratios.
  • Stoichiometry is widely used in laboratory, industrial, environmental, and pharmaceutical applications.
