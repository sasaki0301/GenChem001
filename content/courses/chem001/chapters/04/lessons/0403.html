Calculating Amounts of Reactants and Products

Real-World Scenario

A chemical engineer needs to determine how much ammonia can be produced from a given amount of nitrogen gas. A pharmaceutical manufacturer needs to know exactly how much reactant is required to produce a specific quantity of medicine. These questions are answered using stoichiometry, the branch of chemistry that deals with quantitative relationships in chemical reactions.

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the meaning of stoichiometry.
  • Interpret mole ratios from balanced chemical equations.
  • Perform mole-to-mole calculations.
  • Perform mass-to-mass calculations.
  • Perform mass-to-mole and mole-to-mass calculations.
  • Apply stoichiometric reasoning to chemical reactions.

What is Stoichiometry?

Stoichiometry is the calculation of the amounts of reactants and products in a chemical reaction.

Stoichiometry uses the coefficients in a balanced chemical equation to determine how many moles of one substance react with or produce another substance.

A balanced equation works like a recipe. If a recipe says 2 cups of flour make 12 cookies, then doubling the flour also doubles the cookies. In the same way, chemical coefficients show the proportional relationship between substances.

Balanced Equations as Chemical Recipes

Example Equation

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

This means:

  • 1 mole of N₂ reacts with 3 moles of H₂.
  • 1 mole of N₂ produces 2 moles of NH₃.
  • 3 moles of H₂ produce 2 moles of NH₃.
The coefficients in a balanced equation become the mole ratios used in stoichiometry.

Mole Ratios

A mole ratio is a conversion factor taken from the coefficients of a balanced chemical equation.

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Some possible mole ratios are:

3 mol H₂ 1 mol N₂ or 2 mol NH₃ 1 mol N₂ or 2 mol NH₃ 3 mol H₂

The correct mole ratio depends on what substance is given and what substance is being solved for.

General Stoichiometry Roadmap

Most stoichiometry problems follow this sequence:

  1. Write and balance the chemical equation.
  2. Convert the given quantity to moles, if needed.
  3. Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation.
  4. Convert the answer to the requested unit, if needed.

Mole-to-Mole Calculations

The simplest stoichiometry problems convert from moles of one substance to moles of another substance.

Worked Example 1

How many moles of NH₃ can be produced from 5.00 mol of N₂?

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Step 1: Identify the mole ratio.

2 mol NH₃ 1 mol N₂

Step 2: Multiply by the mole ratio.

5.00 mol N₂ × 2 mol NH₃ 1 mol N₂ = 10.0 mol NH₃
Answer: 10.0 mol NH₃

Mass-to-Mass Calculations

Most laboratory measurements use grams, not moles. For this reason, mass-to-mass stoichiometry is one of the most useful skills in chemistry.

Mass-to-mass pathway:

Mass → Moles → Mole Ratio → Moles → Mass

Worked Example 2: Mass-to-Mass

How many grams of water are produced when 4.00 g of hydrogen reacts completely with oxygen?

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Step 1: Convert grams of H₂ to moles.

4.00 g H₂ × 1 mol H₂ 2.02 g H₂ = 1.98 mol H₂

Step 2: Use the mole ratio.

1.98 mol H₂ × 2 mol H₂O 2 mol H₂ = 1.98 mol H₂O

Step 3: Convert moles of H₂O to grams.

1.98 mol H₂O × 18.02 g H₂O 1 mol H₂O = 35.7 g H₂O
Answer: 35.7 g H₂O

Mole-to-Mass Calculations

In mole-to-mass problems, the given quantity is already in moles, so you only need the mole ratio and molar mass.

Worked Example 3

How many grams of CO₂ are produced from 3.00 mol of CH₄?

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Step 1: Use the mole ratio.

3.00 mol CH₄ × 1 mol CO₂ 1 mol CH₄ = 3.00 mol CO₂

Step 2: Convert moles of CO₂ to grams.

3.00 mol CO₂ × 44.01 g CO₂ 1 mol CO₂ = 132.03 g CO₂
Answer: 132.03 g CO₂

Mass-to-Mole Calculations

In mass-to-mole problems, convert the given mass into moles first, then use the mole ratio.

Worked Example 4

How many moles of oxygen are needed to react completely with 16.0 g of methane?

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Step 1: Convert methane to moles.

16.0 g CH₄ × 1 mol CH₄ 16.05 g CH₄ = 0.997 mol CH₄

Step 2: Use the mole ratio.

0.997 mol CH₄ × 2 mol O₂ 1 mol CH₄ = 1.99 mol O₂
Answer: 1.99 mol O₂

Choosing the Correct Mole Ratio

The mole ratio must cancel the given substance and leave the desired substance.

For the equation:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

If the given substance is H₂ and the target is H₂O, use:

2 mol H₂O 2 mol H₂

This works because mol H₂ cancels, leaving mol H₂O.

Common Mistakes

  • Using an unbalanced chemical equation.
  • Skipping the mole conversion step.
  • Using the wrong mole ratio.
  • Using subscripts instead of coefficients for mole ratios.
  • Forgetting to convert the final answer into the requested unit.
  • Using atomic mass instead of molar mass for compounds.

Check Your Understanding

1. Using the equation N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, how many moles of NH₃ are produced from 4.00 mol N₂?

4.00 mol N₂ × 2 mol NH₃ 1 mol N₂ = 8.00 mol NH₃

2. Using 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, how many moles of H₂O are produced from 6.00 mol H₂?

6.00 mol H₂ × 2 mol H₂O 2 mol H₂ = 6.00 mol H₂O

3. How many grams of CO₂ are produced from 1.00 mol CO₂?

1.00 mol CO₂ × 44.01 g CO₂ 1 mol CO₂ = 44.01 g CO₂

4. Why must every stoichiometric calculation begin with a balanced equation?

Because the coefficients in the balanced equation provide the mole ratios needed for stoichiometric calculations.

5. Which conversion factor connects two substances in a chemical equation?

The mole ratio derived from the coefficients of the balanced chemical equation.

6. Using CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O, how many moles of O₂ are needed for 2.50 mol CH₄?

2.50 mol CH₄ × 2 mol O₂ 1 mol CH₄ = 5.00 mol O₂

7. Using 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂, how many moles of O₂ are produced from 4.00 mol KClO₃?

4.00 mol KClO₃ × 3 mol O₂ 2 mol KClO₃ = 6.00 mol O₂

8. How many grams of NH₃ are produced from 2.00 mol N₂ using N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃?

Step 1: Convert mol N₂ to mol NH₃.

2.00 mol N₂ × 2 mol NH₃ 1 mol N₂ = 4.00 mol NH₃

Step 2: Convert mol NH₃ to grams.

4.00 mol NH₃ × 17.04 g NH₃ 1 mol NH₃ = 68.16 g NH₃

Lesson Summary

  • Stoichiometry calculates the amounts of reactants and products in chemical reactions.
  • Balanced equations provide the mole ratios used in calculations.
  • Mole ratios come from coefficients, not subscripts.
  • Mole-to-mole problems use only the balanced equation ratio.
  • Mass-to-mass problems require conversion from grams to moles and then back to grams.
  • Unit cancellation helps confirm that the setup is correct.
