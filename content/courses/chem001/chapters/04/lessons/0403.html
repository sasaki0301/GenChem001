Empirical Formulas from Composition Data

Percent composition tells us how much of each element is present by mass. How can those masses reveal the simplest ratio of atoms in a compound?

ObjectivesConceptStepsExamplesFractional ratiosPractice

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain an empirical formula as the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  • Find an empirical formula from a molecular formula or a given mole ratio.
  • Determine an empirical formula from measured masses or percent composition.
  • Interpret near-whole-number and common fractional mole ratios without premature rounding.
  • Check whether a proposed formula is consistent with the input composition.
Connection to Lesson 2: We previously moved from a formula to mass percent. Now we reverse the process: composition → masses → moles → simplest whole-number ratio. Lesson 4 will use empirical formulas and molar mass to determine molecular formulas.

1. What does an empirical formula show?

An empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound. It does not necessarily show the actual numbers of atoms in a molecule.

Molecular formula Atom ratio Divide subscripts by Empirical formula
C6H12O6 6 : 12 : 6 6 CH2O
H2O2 2 : 2 2 HO
H2O 2 : 1 1 H2O

An empirical formula and molecular formula can be the same, as for H2O, or different, as for H2O2. Different molecular compounds can share an empirical formula; the ratio alone does not establish the actual molecular formula or structure.

Ionic compounds such as NaCl are represented by formula units, which show the simplest whole-number ratio of ions in the compound. Do not describe an ionic formula as the formula of a discrete molecule.

Try it first

What is the empirical formula corresponding to C4H8?

Show answer

Divide both subscripts by 4: 4 : 8 becomes 1 : 2, so the empirical formula is CH2.

2. From composition data to an empirical formula

  1. Find the mass of each element. If only percentages are given, choose a convenient 100.0 g sample: 40.0% corresponds to 40.0 g in that assumed sample.
  2. Convert mass to moles. For each element, divide its mass in grams by its atomic molar mass in g/mol.
  3. Find a simple mole ratio. Divide every mole amount by the smallest mole amount.
  4. Convert to whole numbers. If the ratios are close to small integers, use those integers. For common fractions such as 1.5, multiply every ratio by the same small whole number.
  5. Write and check the formula. Use the resulting whole-number ratio as subscripts; omit subscript 1. Compare the calculated composition with the given data to the precision available.

moles of element = mass of element ÷ atomic molar mass

Atoms combine in whole-number ratios, while a laboratory sample contains many atoms. This is why the relevant comparison is moles of atoms, not the mass ratio itself.

When starting with actual element masses, use those measured masses directly; you do not have to assume a 100 g sample. If the given composition does not include every element, do not infer missing elements without additional information.

3. Worked examples

For these exercises use C = 12.01, H = 1.01, O = 16.00, Mg = 24.31, and Fe = 55.85 g/mol. Values may differ slightly with another rounding convention.

Example 1 · From percentages to a near-integer ratio

A compound contains 40.00% C, 6.71% H, and 53.29% O by mass. Find the empirical formula.

Assume a 100.0 g sample, so the element masses are 40.00 g C, 6.71 g H, and 53.29 g O.

Element Moles = mass ÷ molar mass Divide by smallest, approximately 3.331
C 40.00 ÷ 12.01 ≈ 3.331 mol 3.331 ÷ 3.331 ≈ 1.00
H 6.71 ÷ 1.01 ≈ 6.644 mol 6.644 ÷ 3.331 ≈ 1.99
O 53.29 ÷ 16.00 ≈ 3.331 mol 3.331 ÷ 3.331 ≈ 1.00

The ratio is approximately 1 : 2 : 1, so the empirical formula is CH2O. The small difference from exactly 2 reflects the precision of the input values.

Check: M(CH2O) = 12.01 + 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 30.03 g/mol. The calculated percentages are about 39.99% C, 6.73% H, and 53.28% O, consistent with the rounded data.

Example 2 · From measured masses

A compound contains only magnesium and oxygen. A sample contains 4.862 g Mg and 3.200 g O.

Mg: 4.862 ÷ 24.31 = 0.2000 mol
O: 3.200 ÷ 16.00 = 0.2000 mol

Divide by 0.2000: Mg : O = 1 : 1. The empirical formula is MgO.

Try it first · From a mole ratio

A sample contains 0.30 mol of element A atoms and 0.60 mol of element B atoms. What is the empirical formula?

Show answer

Divide by the smaller amount, 0.30 mol: A : B = 1 : 2. The empirical formula is AB2.

4. What if the ratio is not a whole number?

Values close to integers because of measurement and rounding may be treated as those integers, but a value near 1.50 should not simply be rounded to 2. Instead, multiply every ratio by the same small integer to obtain a whole-number ratio.

Ratio close to Multiply all ratios by Result for that term
1.5 2 3
1.33 or 1.67 3 approximately 4 or 5
1.25 or 1.75 4 5 or 7

Example 3 · A 1 : 1.5 mole ratio

An iron oxide contains 69.94% Fe and 30.06% O. In a 100.00 g sample:

Fe: 69.94 ÷ 55.85 ≈ 1.252 mol
O: 30.06 ÷ 16.00 ≈ 1.879 mol

Divide by 1.252: Fe : O ≈ 1.00 : 1.50. Multiply both values by 2: 2 : 3. The empirical formula is Fe2O3.

Check: its molar mass using these data is 2(55.85) + 3(16.00) = 159.70 g/mol. Fe percent = 111.70 ÷ 159.70 × 100% ≈ 69.94%; O percent ≈ 30.06%.

Measurements have uncertainty. Ratios such as 1.49 or 1.51 may suggest 1.5 if consistent with the data; do not force an arbitrary ratio to the nearest fraction. If no plausible small whole-number ratio matches, recheck measurements, arithmetic, assumptions, and rounding.

5. Common errors to avoid

Error Better approach
Using percent masses as formula subscripts. Convert each element’s mass to moles before comparing ratios.
Dividing by the largest mole value. Divide every value by the smallest to obtain a convenient starting ratio.
Changing 1 : 1.5 directly to 1 : 2. Multiply both terms by 2 to preserve the ratio: 2 : 3.
Multiplying only the fractional term. Multiply all terms by the same factor.
Assuming an empirical formula identifies one particular molecule. Different molecular formulas can share the same empirical ratio; additional information is needed.

Why it matters: Empirical formulas summarize elemental composition and help chemists compare experimental results with proposed formulas. Composition alone does not uniquely identify a compound’s structure.

Challenge · Start with percentages

A compound contains 27.29% C and 72.71% O by mass. Using C = 12.01 and O = 16.00 g/mol, determine its empirical formula. Assume these are the only elements.

Show one solution

For 100.00 g: C = 27.29 ÷ 12.01 ≈ 2.272 mol; O = 72.71 ÷ 16.00 ≈ 4.544 mol. Divide both by 2.272: C : O ≈ 1 : 2. The empirical formula is CO2. Check: using the same masses, % C = 12.01 ÷ 44.01 × 100% ≈ 27.29%.

7. Key takeaways

  • An empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms.
  • Convert element masses to moles before comparing their quantities.
  • Divide all mole values by the smallest, then multiply all ratios by a common factor if needed.
  • Keep extra digits and check the final formula against the given data.
  • An empirical formula alone does not uniquely determine a molecular formula or structure.
