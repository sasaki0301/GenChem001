Molecular Formulas from Empirical Formulas

A compound has the empirical formula CH2O, but that ratio can describe molecules of different sizes. How can molar mass help us find the molecular formula?

ObjectivesCompare formulasStepsExamplesFrom percent dataPractice

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish an empirical formula from a molecular formula.
  • Calculate the molar mass corresponding to an empirical formula.
  • Use measured molar mass to find the whole-number multiplier and molecular formula.
  • Apply percent-composition data and molar mass together to determine a molecular formula.
  • Check a result and explain what information a molecular formula does and does not provide.
Connection to Lesson 3: Convert composition data to moles and simplify the ratio to find an empirical formula. In this lesson, a compound’s molar mass supplies the additional information needed to find its molecular formula.

1. Empirical formula versus molecular formula

Formula What it shows Example
Empirical formula The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of the elements. CH2O gives C : H : O = 1 : 2 : 1.
Molecular formula The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. C6H12O6 has 6 C, 12 H, and 6 O atoms per molecule.

All molecular-formula subscripts are a common positive whole-number multiple of the empirical-formula subscripts. The multiplier can be 1, in which case both formulas are the same. For example, H2O has an empirical and molecular formula of H2O.

CH2O and C6H12O6 share the same simplest ratio, but they represent different numbers of atoms per molecule. A molecular formula also does not uniquely show how atoms are connected: different structural isomers can share one molecular formula.

This lesson applies molecular formulas to substances made of discrete molecules. Ionic compounds are described by formula units, not by discrete molecular formulas.

2. Find the molecular formula

Use the atomic molar masses C = 12.01, H = 1.01, O = 16.00, and N = 14.01 g/mol for the examples and practice in this lesson.

  1. Determine or identify the empirical formula.
  2. Calculate its empirical-formula molar mass by adding the atomic molar-mass contributions of all its subscripts.
  3. Divide the compound’s measured molecular molar mass by the empirical-formula molar mass.
  4. Interpret the quotient as a positive whole-number multiplier, allowing for reasonable measurement and rounding differences.
  5. Multiply every empirical-formula subscript by that multiplier and check the resulting molar mass.

n = molecular molar mass ÷ empirical-formula molar mass
molecular formula = (empirical formula)n

The notation above means multiply each subscript by n. For instance, (CH2O)3 gives C3H6O3; the 1 for C and O is also multiplied.

The quotient has no unit because both masses are in g/mol. If it is not reasonably close to a positive whole number, recheck the empirical formula, arithmetic, units, and measured data. Do not force an inconsistent value to a whole number.

3. Worked examples

Example 1 · Empirical formula CH2O

A molecular compound has empirical formula CH2O and measured molar mass 180.18 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

  1. Empirical-formula molar mass = 12.01 + 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 30.03 g/mol.
  2. n = 180.18 ÷ 30.03 = 6.
  3. Multiply every subscript by 6: (CH2O)6 = C6H12O6.
  4. Check: 6(12.01) + 12(1.01) + 6(16.00) = 180.18 g/mol.

Example 2 · When n = 1

A molecular compound has empirical formula NO2 and measured molar mass 46.01 g/mol.

Empirical-formula molar mass = 14.01 + 2(16.00) = 46.01 g/mol.
n = 46.01 ÷ 46.01 = 1. The molecular formula is NO2.

Example 3 · Multiply an implicit subscript

A molecular compound has empirical formula CH2 and measured molar mass 42.09 g/mol.

Empirical-formula molar mass = 12.01 + 2(1.01) = 14.03 g/mol.
n = 42.09 ÷ 14.03 = 3. Multiply both subscripts: C1×3H2×3 = C3H6.

Check: 3(12.01) + 6(1.01) = 42.09 g/mol.

Try it first

A molecular compound has empirical formula HO and measured molar mass 34.02 g/mol. What is its molecular formula?

Show answer

Empirical-formula molar mass = 1.01 + 16.00 = 17.01 g/mol. n = 34.02 ÷ 17.01 = 2, so the molecular formula is H2O2.

4. From percent composition to molecular formula

Example 4 · Combine Lessons 3 and 4

A compound contains only C, H, and O: 40.00% C, 6.71% H, and 53.29% O by mass. Its measured molar mass is 180.18 g/mol. Determine its molecular formula.

  1. For an assumed 100.00 g sample, the masses are 40.00 g C, 6.71 g H, and 53.29 g O.
  2. Convert to moles: C ≈ 40.00 ÷ 12.01 = 3.331 mol; H ≈ 6.71 ÷ 1.01 = 6.644 mol; O ≈ 53.29 ÷ 16.00 = 3.331 mol.
  3. Divide by the smallest amount: C : H : O ≈ 1.00 : 1.99 : 1.00 ≈ 1 : 2 : 1. The empirical formula is CH2O.
  4. Its empirical-formula molar mass is 30.03 g/mol. n = 180.18 ÷ 30.03 = 6.
  5. The molecular formula is C6H12O6; its calculated molar mass is 180.18 g/mol.

Small differences in the composition check are expected from rounded data: CH2O gives approximately 39.99% C, 6.73% H, and 53.28% O with these atomic masses.

Knowing this molecular formula does not prove the substance is a particular named compound: molecules with the same formula can have different structures. More evidence is needed to identify the structure.

5. Check your answer and avoid common errors

Check or mistake What to do
Using percentages as subscripts Convert to element moles, then simplify the mole ratio first.
Using molar mass divided by one atomic molar mass Divide by the molar mass of the complete empirical formula.
Multiplying only one subscript Apply n to every element, including any implicit subscript 1.
Forcing a quotient far from an integer Recheck arithmetic, units, empirical formula, and precision of the data.
Claiming a unique structure from a molecular formula Recognize that structural isomers can share a molecular formula.

Always verify that the calculated molecular molar mass agrees with the given value within its measurement precision, and that reducing the subscripts returns the empirical formula.

Challenge · Work from composition and molar mass

A compound contains only carbon and hydrogen: 85.60% C and 14.40% H by mass. Its molecular molar mass is 56.12 g/mol. Determine its molecular formula using C = 12.01 and H = 1.01 g/mol.

Show one solution

Assume 100.00 g: C ≈ 85.60 ÷ 12.01 = 7.13 mol; H ≈ 14.40 ÷ 1.01 = 14.26 mol. Divide by 7.13 to obtain approximately 1 : 2, giving empirical formula CH2. Its empirical-formula molar mass is 14.03 g/mol. n = 56.12 ÷ 14.03 = 4, so the molecular formula is C4H8. Check: 4(12.01) + 8(1.01) = 56.12 g/mol. Other evidence is needed to identify its structure.

7. Key takeaways

  • Empirical formulas show simplest atom ratios; molecular formulas show atom counts in individual molecules.
  • n = measured molecular molar mass ÷ empirical-formula molar mass.
  • Multiply every empirical-formula subscript by the same positive whole number n.
  • n can be 1; check both the formula ratio and calculated molar mass.
  • A molecular formula does not uniquely determine molecular structure.
