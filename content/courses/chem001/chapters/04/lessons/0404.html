Exploring Limiting Reactant and Percent Yield

Real-World Scenario

A pharmaceutical company combines two reactants to produce a medicine. Although the reaction is designed to produce 100 g of product, only 85 g is actually obtained. Why didn't the reaction produce the expected amount? In real chemical reactions, one reactant is often used up first, stopping the reaction. In addition, some product may be lost during processing. Chemists use the concepts of limiting reactants, theoretical yield, and percent yield to understand and predict reaction efficiency.

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the limiting reactant in a chemical reaction.
  • Determine the excess reactant.
  • Calculate theoretical yield.
  • Calculate percent yield.
  • Interpret reaction efficiency using percent yield.

What is a Limiting Reactant?

A limiting reactant is the reactant that is completely consumed first in a chemical reaction.

The limiting reactant determines the maximum amount of product that can be formed.

Once the limiting reactant is used up, the reaction stops even if other reactants remain.

Cookie Analogy

Suppose one batch of cookies requires:

  • 2 eggs
  • 3 cups of flour

If you have:

  • 10 eggs
  • 9 cups flour

The flour runs out first, so flour is the limiting ingredient.

The same idea applies to chemical reactions.

Limiting vs Excess Reactant

Term Description
Limiting Reactant Reactant consumed completely first
Excess Reactant Reactant remaining after the reaction stops
Only the limiting reactant determines the amount of product formed.

Determining the Limiting Reactant

The most reliable method is to calculate how much product each reactant can produce.

The reactant that produces the smaller amount of product is the limiting reactant.

Worked Example 1

Consider the reaction:

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Suppose we have:

  • 2.0 mol N₂
  • 4.0 mol H₂

Step 1: Determine NH₃ from N₂.

2.0 mol N₂ × 2 mol NH₃ 1 mol N₂ = 4.0 mol NH₃

Step 2: Determine NH₃ from H₂.

4.0 mol H₂ × 2 mol NH₃ 3 mol H₂ = 2.67 mol NH₃

Because H₂ produces the smaller amount of NH₃, hydrogen is the limiting reactant.

Limiting Reactant: H₂
Maximum Product: 2.67 mol NH₃

Theoretical Yield

The theoretical yield is the maximum amount of product that can be produced based on stoichiometric calculations.

Theoretical Yield = Maximum possible product predicted by chemistry

Theoretical yield assumes:

  • Perfect reaction conditions
  • No side reactions
  • No product loss
  • 100% conversion of limiting reactant

Actual Yield

The actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained in the laboratory or industrial process.

Actual Yield = Product actually collected

Actual yield is almost always lower than theoretical yield because of product losses and incomplete reactions.

Percent Yield

Percent yield measures the efficiency of a reaction.

Actual Yield Theoretical Yield × 100%

Worked Example 2

A reaction has:

  • Theoretical Yield = 50.0 g
  • Actual Yield = 42.5 g

Calculate the percent yield.

42.5 g 50.0 g × 100 = 85.0%
Percent Yield = 85.0%

Why Percent Yield is Usually Less Than 100%

  • Incomplete reactions
  • Product losses during separation
  • Side reactions producing unwanted products
  • Experimental errors
  • Impurities in reactants
Most industrial processes aim for the highest percent yield possible because higher yields reduce waste and increase profit.

Industrial Applications

Industry Application
Pharmaceuticals Maximizing medicine production

Food Processing Reducing raw material waste
Manufacturing Optimizing chemical processes

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming the reactant with the smallest amount is always limiting.
  • Failing to compare product amounts from each reactant.
  • Using actual yield instead of theoretical yield in stoichiometric calculations.
  • Forgetting to multiply by 100 when calculating percent yield.
  • Using an unbalanced equation.

Check Your Understanding

2. What is an excess reactant?

A reactant that remains after the reaction stops.

3. What is theoretical yield?

The maximum amount of product predicted by stoichiometric calculations.

72 80 × 100 = 90%

Because of incomplete reactions, side reactions, product losses, and experimental limitations.

Lesson Summary

  • The limiting reactant is consumed first and determines product formation.
  • The excess reactant remains after the reaction ends.
  • Theoretical yield is the maximum predicted product.
  • Actual yield is the amount of product actually obtained.
  • Percent yield measures reaction efficiency.
  • Limiting reactant and percent yield calculations are essential in laboratory and industrial chemistry.
