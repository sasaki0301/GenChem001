Percent Composition of Compounds

Water contains more hydrogen atoms than oxygen atoms, yet oxygen contributes most of its mass. How can we calculate the share contributed by each element?

Learning objectives

After this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain percent composition as the percent by mass contributed by each element.
  • Determine each element’s mass contribution and calculate percent composition from a chemical formula.
  • Calculate percent by mass from measured sample data and find an element’s mass from a known percentage.
  • Check whether results are reasonable and explain why both atomic mass and the number of atoms matter.
Connect to Lesson 1: Count atoms from the chemical formula, multiply by their atomic molar masses, and add the contributions to find the compound’s molar mass. Those contributions now become the parts of the whole.

1. What does percent composition mean?

Percent composition by mass tells what fraction of a compound’s total mass comes from each element, expressed as a percentage.

mass percent of an element = (mass of element ÷ mass of compound) × 100%

For example, if a pure sample contains 8.0 g of element X in 20.0 g of compound, X accounts for (8.0 ÷ 20.0) × 100% = 40.0% by mass. In a 100 g sample of the same pure compound, about 40.0 g would be X.

Mass percent is not the percentage of atoms. Subscripts show atom ratios; the mass share depends on both the number of atoms and the atomic molar mass of each element.

For a complete pure compound, the mass percentages of all its constituent elements total approximately 100%. Small differences can arise when individual answers are rounded.

2. Calculate percent composition from a chemical formula

Use the rounded atomic molar masses below, consistent with Lesson 1. Results may vary slightly with other periodic-table values.

Element H C N O Na Mg S Cl Ca
g/mol 1.01 12.01 14.01 16.00 22.99 24.31 32.06 35.45 40.08
  1. Read the formula and count the atoms of each element per molecule or formula unit.
  2. Calculate each element’s mass contribution in 1 mol of compound: number of atoms × atomic molar mass.
  3. Add those contributions to obtain the compound’s molar mass.
  4. Divide the contribution of each element by the total molar mass and multiply by 100%.
  5. Add the percentages to check the result; keep extra digits until the final rounding.

% element = [(atoms of element in formula × atomic molar mass) ÷ compound molar mass] × 100%

The numerator and denominator both describe masses for the same amount: one mole of compound. Their units cancel before the ratio is converted to a percentage.

3. Worked examples

Example 1 · Water, H2O

Count 2 H atoms and 1 O atom. Find the contributions for 1 mol of H2O:

Element Mass contribution per mol H2O Percent by mass
H 2 × 1.01 = 2.02 g (2.02 ÷ 18.02) × 100% = 11.21%
O 1 × 16.00 = 16.00 g (16.00 ÷ 18.02) × 100% = 88.79%
Total 18.02 g; M = 18.02 g/mol 100.00%

Oxygen supplies the larger mass contribution: 16.00 g compared with 2.02 g in one mole of water.

Example 2 · Calcium carbonate, CaCO3

For one mole: Ca = 1 × 40.08 = 40.08 g; C = 1 × 12.01 = 12.01 g; O = 3 × 16.00 = 48.00 g. Total molar mass = 100.09 g/mol.

Element Calculation Mass percent
Ca (40.08 ÷ 100.09) × 100% 40.04%
C (12.01 ÷ 100.09) × 100% 12.00%
O (48.00 ÷ 100.09) × 100% 47.96%

Check: 40.04% + 12.00% + 47.96% = 100.00%. Although one Ca atom is heavier than one O atom, three O atoms contribute more mass than one Ca atom here.

Example 3 · Parentheses in magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)2

Count Mg = 1, O = 2, H = 2. Contributions: Mg = 24.31 g; O = 32.00 g; H = 2.02 g per mole of compound. M = 24.31 + 32.00 + 2.02 = 58.33 g/mol.

% Mg = 24.31 ÷ 58.33 × 100% = 41.68%
% O = 32.00 ÷ 58.33 × 100% = 54.86%
% H = 2.02 ÷ 58.33 × 100% = 3.46%

Check: 41.68% + 54.86% + 3.46% = 100.00%.

4. Work with a measured sample

Example 4 · From masses to percentages

A pure 25.0 g sample of a compound contains 10.0 g of element X and 15.0 g of element Y. The two elements account for the full sample.

% X = (10.0 g ÷ 25.0 g) × 100% = 40.0%
% Y = (15.0 g ÷ 25.0 g) × 100% = 60.0%.

Example 5 · From percent to mass

A pure 50.0 g sample of a compound is 32.0% oxygen by mass. Find its oxygen mass.

Oxygen mass = (32.0 ÷ 100) × 50.0 g = 16.0 g. The remaining 34.0 g is due to the other element or elements.

Try it first

A 40.0 g sample of a pure compound is 25.0% element A by mass. How many grams of A are present?

Show answer

Mass of A = 0.250 × 40.0 g = 10.0 g.

5. Check your reasoning

Common mistake Better approach
Using subscripts as mass percentages. Multiply each atom count by its atomic molar mass before dividing by the whole.
Forgetting an outside subscript in a formula such as Mg(OH)2. Apply the 2 to both O and H inside the parentheses.
Using the atomic molar mass of oxygen only once for CO2. Count two O atoms: oxygen contributes 2 × 16.00 g per mole of CO2.
Using the heaviest element alone to predict the largest contribution. Compare count × atomic molar mass for each element. A lighter element can contribute more if enough atoms are present.
Mixing the mass of an element from one sample with the total mass of another. Both parts of the ratio must refer to the same sample or the same amount of compound.

Why it matters: Percent composition can help compare compounds, interpret chemical analyses, and check whether data are consistent with a proposed formula. Matching percentages alone may not uniquely identify a substance.

Challenge · Analyze carbon dioxide

Using C = 12.01 and O = 16.00 g/mol, calculate the mass percentage of carbon and oxygen in CO2. Then determine the mass of oxygen in a pure 100.0 g sample.

Show one solution

M = 12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01 g/mol. % C = 12.01 ÷ 44.01 × 100% ≈ 27.29%; % O = 32.00 ÷ 44.01 × 100% ≈ 72.71%. The percentages add to 100.00% after rounding. Oxygen in 100.0 g ≈ (32.00 ÷ 44.01) × 100.0 g = 72.71 g.

7. Key takeaways

  • Percent composition is the mass of each element divided by the mass of the compound, multiplied by 100%.
  • From a formula, first calculate each element’s contribution per mole and the total molar mass.
  • Use the same sample or amount for the numerator and denominator.
  • Atom counts and atomic molar masses both affect the mass contribution.
  • All element percentages in a complete pure compound add to approximately 100%; small differences may come from rounding.
  • Element mass = sample mass × element’s mass fraction.
