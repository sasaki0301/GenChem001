Chapter Summary: Gases

Chapter Big Idea

Gases behave differently from solids and liquids because their particles are constantly moving, widely separated, and experience minimal attractive forces. The behavior of gases can be explained using the Kinetic Molecular Theory and predicted quantitatively using gas laws. This chapter explored the properties of gases, the relationships among pressure, volume, temperature, and amount of gas, and the mathematical models used to predict gas behavior in real-world applications.

1. Properties of Gases

Gases differ significantly from solids and liquids because of the motion and arrangement of their particles.

Property Description
No Definite Shape Gases take the shape of their container
No Definite Volume Gases expand to fill available space
Compressibility Gases can be compressed significantly
Low Density Particles are far apart
Diffusion Spontaneous mixing of gas particles
Effusion Escape of gas through a tiny opening
The unique behavior of gases results from the large distances between particles and their constant motion.

2. Kinetic Molecular Theory (KMT)

The Kinetic Molecular Theory explains gas behavior based on particle motion.

Major Assumptions of KMT:
  1. Gases consist of tiny particles.
  2. Particles move in constant random motion.
  3. Collisions are elastic.
  4. Particles occupy negligible volume.
  5. There are minimal attractive forces between particles.
  6. Average kinetic energy depends on temperature.

KMT explains why gases expand, diffuse, exert pressure, and respond to changes in temperature.

3. Gas Pressure

Gas pressure results from collisions of gas particles with the walls of a container.

Pressure is the force exerted per unit area.

Pressure increases when:

  • Temperature increases
  • More gas particles are added
  • Volume decreases

4. The Four Variables of Gases

Variable Symbol Common Units
Pressure P atm, kPa, mmHg
Volume V L, mL
Temperature T K
Amount of Gas n mol
All gas laws describe relationships among these four variables.

5. Temperature and the Kelvin Scale

Gas law calculations require temperature to be expressed in Kelvin.

K = °C + 273.15

Examples

0°C = 273 K

25°C = 298 K

100°C = 373 K
Never use Celsius values directly in gas law equations.

6. Boyle's Law

Boyle's Law describes the relationship between pressure and volume when temperature and moles remain constant.

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
Pressure and volume are inversely proportional.
Change Result
Pressure ↑ Volume ↓
Pressure ↓ Volume ↑

7. Charles's Law

Charles's Law describes the relationship between volume and temperature when pressure and moles remain constant.

V₁ T₁ = V₂ T₂
Volume and temperature are directly proportional.

8. Gay-Lussac's Law

Gay-Lussac's Law describes the relationship between pressure and temperature when volume and moles remain constant.

P₁ T₁ = P₂ T₂
Pressure and temperature are directly proportional.

9. Avogadro's Law

Avogadro's Law describes the relationship between volume and the number of moles.

V₁ n₁ = V₂ n₂
Volume and number of moles are directly proportional.

10. Summary of Individual Gas Laws

Gas Law Variables Relationship
Boyle's Law P and V Inverse
Charles's Law V and T Direct
Gay-Lussac's Law P and T Direct
Avogadro's Law V and n Direct

11. The Combined Gas Law

The Combined Gas Law combines Boyle's, Charles's, and Gay-Lussac's Laws into a single equation.

P₁V₁ T₁ = P₂V₂ T₂
Use the Combined Gas Law when pressure, volume, and temperature all change while the number of moles remains constant.

12. The Ideal Gas Law

The Ideal Gas Law combines all major gas variables into one equation.

PV = nRT
Symbol Meaning
P Pressure
V Volume
n Moles
R Gas Constant
T Temperature (K)
The Ideal Gas Law is used when pressure, volume, temperature, and moles are involved.

13. Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP)

Variable Value
Temperature 273 K (0°C)
Pressure 1 atm
At STP:

1 mole of an ideal gas occupies approximately 22.4 L.
1 mol gas = 22.4 L at STP

Chapter Mastery Check

1. Why are gases highly compressible?

Because large empty spaces exist between gas particles.

2. What causes gas pressure?

Collisions of gas particles with container walls.

3. Which gas law describes the inverse relationship between pressure and volume?

Boyle's Law

4. Which gas law uses the equation PV = nRT?

Ideal Gas Law

5. What volume does one mole of an ideal gas occupy at STP?

22.4 L

6. Why must Kelvin be used in gas law calculations?

Gas laws require absolute temperature measurements.

Chapter Summary

  • Gases have no definite shape or volume and are highly compressible.
  • The Kinetic Molecular Theory explains gas behavior through particle motion.
  • Gas pressure results from collisions between particles and container walls.
  • Boyle's Law describes the inverse relationship between pressure and volume.
  • Charles's Law describes the direct relationship between volume and temperature.
  • Gay-Lussac's Law describes the direct relationship between pressure and temperature.
  • Avogadro's Law describes the direct relationship between volume and moles.
  • The Combined Gas Law relates pressure, volume, and temperature.
  • The Ideal Gas Law combines pressure, volume, temperature, and moles into one equation.
  • At STP, one mole of an ideal gas occupies approximately 22.4 L.
  • Gas laws are widely applied in science, engineering, medicine, meteorology, and industry.
