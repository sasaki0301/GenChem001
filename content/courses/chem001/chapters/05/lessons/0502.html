Gas Laws

Real-World Scenario

Why does a syringe become harder to push when the opening is blocked? Why does a balloon shrink when placed in a freezer? Why do vehicle tire pressures increase on hot days? These everyday observations are explained by gas laws. Gas laws describe the mathematical relationships among pressure, volume, temperature, and the amount of gas. Understanding these relationships allows scientists and engineers to predict gas behavior in laboratories, industrial systems, engines, refrigeration units, weather forecasting, and aerospace applications.

The Four Variables of Gases

Gas behavior can be described using four major variables:

Variable Symbol Common Units
Pressure P atm, kPa, mmHg
Volume V L, mL
Temperature T K (Kelvin)
Amount of Gas n mol
Gas laws examine how one variable changes when another variable changes.

Temperature Must Be in Kelvin

All gas law calculations involving temperature must use the Kelvin scale.

K = °C + 273.15

Examples

25°C = 298 K

0°C = 273 K

100°C = 373 K
Never substitute Celsius values directly into gas law equations.

Boyle's Law

In 1662, Robert Boyle discovered that pressure and volume are inversely related when temperature and the amount of gas remain constant.

As volume decreases, pressure increases.

As volume increases, pressure decreases.
P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
Variable Held Constant Relationship
Temperature and moles Pressure ∝ 1/Volume

Boyle's Law Example

A gas occupies 4.0 L at 1.5 atm. What volume will it occupy at 3.0 atm?

Given:

  • P₁ = 1.5 atm
  • V₁ = 4.0 L
  • P₂ = 3.0 atm
  • V₂ = ?
V₂ = P₁V₁ P₂
V₂ = (1.5)(4.0) 3.0 = 2.0 L

Charles's Law

Jacques Charles discovered that volume and temperature are directly proportional when pressure and the amount of gas remain constant.

As temperature increases, volume increases.

As temperature decreases, volume decreases.
V₁ T₁ = V₂ T₂

Charles's Law Example

A balloon occupies 3.0 L at 300 K. What volume will it occupy at 450 K?

V₂ = V₁T₂ T₁
V₂ = (3.0)(450) 300 = 4.5 L

Gay-Lussac's Law

Joseph Gay-Lussac found that pressure and temperature are directly proportional when volume and the amount of gas remain constant.

As temperature increases, pressure increases.

As temperature decreases, pressure decreases.
P₁ T₁ = P₂ T₂

Gay-Lussac's Law Example

A gas has a pressure of 1.20 atm at 300 K. What pressure will it have at 450 K?

P₂ = P₁T₂ T₁
P₂ = (1.20)(450) 300 = 1.80 atm

Avogadro's Law

Amedeo Avogadro proposed that equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of particles.

Volume is directly proportional to the number of moles.
V₁ n₁ = V₂ n₂

Avogadro's Law Example

A gas occupies 5.0 L and contains 0.50 mol. What volume will it occupy if the amount increases to 1.50 mol?

V₂ = V₁n₂ n₁
V₂ = (5.0)(1.50) 0.50 = 15.0 L

Common Mistakes

  • Using Celsius instead of Kelvin.
  • Forgetting which variables remain constant.
  • Confusing direct and inverse relationships.
  • Substituting values into the wrong gas law formula.
  • Failing to check units before calculations.

Check Your Understanding

1. A gas occupies 8.0 L at 2.0 atm. What volume will it occupy at 4.0 atm?

V₂ = 4.0 L
Pressure doubles, so volume is reduced by half.

2. Why must Kelvin be used in gas law calculations?

Gas laws are based on absolute temperature. Using Celsius can produce incorrect results.

3. According to Charles's Law, what happens to volume when temperature increases?

Volume increases because volume and temperature are directly proportional.

4. According to Gay-Lussac's Law, what happens to pressure when temperature increases at constant volume?

Pressure increases because pressure and temperature are directly proportional.

5. A gas occupies 6.0 L at 1.0 mol. What volume will it occupy at 2.0 mol?

V₂ = 12.0 L
Doubling the number of moles doubles the volume.

6. Which gas law explains why a balloon expands when heated?

Charles's Law
