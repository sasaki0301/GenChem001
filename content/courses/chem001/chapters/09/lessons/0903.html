Basic Functional Groups

Real-World Scenario

The scent of flowers, the flavor of fruits, the alcohol in hand sanitizers, the solvents used in laboratories, and many medicines all owe their unique properties to specific groups of atoms called functional groups. Instead of memorizing every organic compound, chemists identify these functional groups because they determine how molecules behave and react. Understanding functional groups allows chemists, engineers, pharmacists, and biologists to predict the properties and uses of millions of organic compounds.

Learning Objectives

  • Define a functional group.
  • Identify the most common functional groups found in organic compounds.
  • Recognize the general structure of each functional group.
  • Relate functional groups to the properties and uses of everyday substances.

What is a Functional Group?

A functional group is a specific atom or group of atoms attached to a carbon skeleton that determines the characteristic properties and chemical reactions of an organic compound.

Think of the carbon skeleton as the "framework" of the molecule, while the functional group acts like a "special feature" that determines how the molecule behaves.

Different molecules may have similar carbon skeletons but behave very differently because they contain different functional groups.

Alcohols (–OH)

Alcohols contain the hydroxyl group (–OH) attached to a carbon atom.

R—OH
The hydroxyl group makes alcohols more polar than hydrocarbons and allows many alcohols to dissolve in water.

Examples

Compound Formula Common Use
Methanol CH₃OH Fuel, industrial solvent
Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH Alcoholic beverages, disinfectants, fuel
Isopropyl Alcohol C₃H₇OH Hand sanitizers and rubbing alcohol
Characteristics
  • Contain the hydroxyl (–OH) group.
  • Generally polar.
  • Can form hydrogen bonds.
  • Often have relatively high boiling points compared to hydrocarbons.

Ethers (–O–)

Ethers contain an oxygen atom bonded to two carbon atoms.

R—O—R'
Unlike alcohols, ethers do not contain an O–H bond, so they cannot form hydrogen bonds with one another.

Examples

Compound Formula Common Use
Dimethyl Ether CH₃OCH₃ Aerosol propellant
Diethyl Ether C₂H₅OC₂H₅ Laboratory solvent
Methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) C₅H₁₂O Gasoline additive
Characteristics
  • Contain one oxygen atom between two carbon groups.
  • Generally less polar than alcohols.
  • Good organic solvents.
  • Usually have lower boiling points than comparable alcohols.

Aldehydes (–CHO)

Aldehydes contain a carbonyl group located at the end of a carbon chain.

R—CHO
The carbonyl carbon is always located at the end of the molecule.

Examples

Compound Formula Common Use
Formaldehyde HCHO Preservative, resin production
Acetaldehyde CH₃CHO Chemical manufacturing
Vanillin C₈H₈O₃ Vanilla flavoring
Characteristics
  • Contain a terminal carbonyl group.
  • Generally reactive.
  • Often possess distinctive odors.
  • Important in fragrances and food flavorings.

Ketones (>C=O)

Ketones contain a carbonyl group bonded to two carbon atoms.

R—CO—R'
Unlike aldehydes, the carbonyl carbon of a ketone is located within the carbon chain rather than at the end.

Examples

Compound Formula Common Use
Acetone CH₃COCH₃ Nail polish remover
Butanone (MEK) C₄H₈O Industrial solvent
Cyclohexanone C₆H₁₀O Nylon production
Characteristics
  • Contain an internal carbonyl group.
  • Excellent organic solvents.
  • Usually have pleasant or sweet odors.
  • Commonly used in paints, coatings, and plastics.
